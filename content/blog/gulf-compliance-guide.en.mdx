# Regulatory Compliance for Apps in the Gulf: The Complete Guide

**If your app processes personal data of people in Saudi Arabia, the UAE or Qatar, at least one data protection law applies to you, and in Saudi Arabia and the UAE that is true wherever your company is registered.** The three main laws are Saudi Arabia's Personal Data Protection Law (enforced by SDAIA), the UAE's Federal Decree-Law 45 of 2021 (with separate regimes in DIFC and ADGM), and Qatar's Law No. 13 of 2016. Sector rules for health, finance and government stack on top of them, not instead of them.

> Written from an engineering perspective, not legal advice. Current as of November 2026.

This is the map: which regime applies, where they agree, where they differ, and what to ask a lawyer. Each country and sector has its own detailed guide linked from here.

---

## Does any of this apply to you?

Work through these in order. The first "yes" brings compliance into scope.

**1. Do you have users in a Gulf country?** Saudi Arabia's law covers processing by an entity outside the Kingdom of data about people residing there (Article 2 of the [PDPL](https://dgp.sdaia.gov.sa/wps/portal/pdp/knowledgecenter/details/PDPL)). The UAE government portal describes the federal law as covering processing ["inside or outside the country"](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/digital-uae/data/data-protection-laws). Qatar's scope article defines coverage by how data is processed and does not address foreign companies directly; ask counsel how it applies to you.

**2. Do you collect anything that identifies a person?** Name, email, phone, location, device identifier, or any combination that identifies someone. An analytics SDK collecting device IDs is processing personal data.

**3. Is any of it sensitive or "special"?** The lists differ by country. Saudi Arabia's covers health, genetic and biometric data, among others, but not financial data. Qatar's "special nature" list includes children's and health data. Sensitive data carries stricter rules everywhere.

**4. Are you in a licensed sector?** Health, finance and government work carry sector licensing **in addition to** data protection law. Complying with a data protection law does not satisfy a health authority or a central bank.

If you answered yes to 1 and 2, keep reading. If you also answered yes to 3 or 4, you need a local adviser, not just this guide.

## The regimes, compared

| | **Saudi Arabia** | **UAE (federal)** | **Qatar** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main law | Personal Data Protection Law | Federal Decree-Law 45 of 2021, in force 2 January 2022 | Law No. 13 of 2016 |
| Regulator | SDAIA | UAE Data Office | National Cyber Governance and Assurance Affairs (NCSA) |
| Reaches companies abroad | Yes (Article 2) | Covers processing inside or outside the UAE | Not addressed in the scope article; ask counsel |
| Bases other than consent | Yes, including legitimate interest for non-sensitive data (Article 6) | Yes, exceptions such as public interest and legal procedures | Yes, a "lawful purpose" of the controller (Article 4) |
| Breach notice to regulator | Within 72 hours of awareness (Implementing Regulation, Art. 24) | No hour count we could verify; ask counsel | Within 72 hours where the breach could cause damage (NCGAA guideline) |
| Transfers abroad | Adequacy list, contractual clauses, binding rules or certificates | Law sets transfer requirements; confirm the conditions with counsel | Controllers may not restrict cross-border flows except where processing breaches the law or risks serious damage (Article 15) |
| Maximum fine | SAR 5 million, doubled for repeats (Article 36) | Not verified in a primary source | QAR 5 million (Article 24) |
| Separate free-zone regimes | — | **DIFC and ADGM** | **QFC** |

Sources for each row: the Saudi [law](https://dgp.sdaia.gov.sa/wps/portal/pdp/knowledgecenter/details/PDPL) and [Implementing Regulation](https://dgp.sdaia.gov.sa/wps/portal/pdp/knowledgecenter/details/PDPL2); the [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/digital-uae/data/data-protection-laws); Qatar's [Law No. 13 of 2016](https://ncsa.gov.qa/shared/document_library/94787172324409/33000/8821/1.0~735ebb9c-e29e-4acb-a7ed-96449884a20b?mimetype=application%2Fpdf&filename=Law+No.+%2813%29+of+2016+on+Protecting+Personal+Data+Privacy.pdf) as published by NCSA and its [breach notification guideline](https://ncsa.gov.qa/shared/document_library/94787172324409/33000/8870/1.0~740b1248-e3a9-40e1-b66e-589091cc182a?mimetype=application%2Fpdf&filename=Personal+Data+Breach+Notifications+-+Guideline+for+Regulated+Entities+%284%29.pdf) (version 2.0, September 2022).

**Three takeaways:**

**The free zones are separate legal regimes.** A company incorporated in DIFC follows the [DIFC Data Protection Law](https://www.difc.com/business/registrars-and-commissioners/commissioner-of-data-protection/faqs-glossary), with its own Commissioner. ADGM has its own [Data Protection Regulations 2021](https://www.adgm.com/operating-in-adgm/office-of-data-protection/data-breach-notifications) and Office of Data Protection, and the Qatar Financial Centre has its own [Data Protection Regulations and Data Protection Office](https://www.qfc.qa/en/operating-with-qfc/data-protection). "We're in Dubai" does not tell you which law governs you; your trade licence does.

**Saudi Arabia has the most detailed transfer rules, Qatar the most transfer-friendly text.** Saudi transfers need a route under SDAIA's transfer regulation, and a risk assessment for sensitive or continuous transfers. Qatar's Article 15 starts from the opposite direction: it stops controllers from restricting cross-border flows unless the processing breaches the law or may cause serious damage.

**The UAE federal picture has gaps we could not close from primary sources.** We could not verify a published breach deadline or fine schedule for the federal law as of November 2026. Ask a UAE adviser: have the Executive Regulations been issued, and do they set a notification period and fines? Until you have an answer, build to the tightest deadline you face elsewhere. ## What all three require

Beneath the differences, the engineering converges. Build these and you have the foundation in every jurisdiction:

- **A lawful basis per processing activity.** Consent where it is the basis: specific, unticked, withdrawable and logged. Saudi Arabia and Qatar both let users withdraw consent at any time.
- **Data subject rights in the product.** Access, a copy, correction and deletion appear in both the Saudi law (Article 4) and Qatar's (Articles 5 and 6). Saudi Arabia sets a 30-day response time.
- **Breach detection and a written procedure.** You cannot report within 72 hours a breach you discover in two months.
- **Purpose limitation and retention.** Keep data only as long as its purpose lasts. Saudi Article 18 and Qatar Article 10 both say so.
- **Security appropriate to the data.** Encryption, access control and audit logging that are actually implemented.
- **A privacy notice that matches reality.** Qatar's Article 9 requires telling individuals who you are and why you process their data before processing starts; Saudi Arabia requires a privacy policy before collection (Article 12).

For the implementation detail, the [Saudi PDPL developer guide](/en/blog/pdpl-compliance-guide) is the most complete, and most of it carries over.

## Data residency: the constraint that shapes architecture

Residency cannot be retrofitted cheaply, which is why it belongs in the first architecture conversation.

- **Saudi Arabia:** transfers are allowed through an adequacy list, standard contractual clauses, binding common rules or an accreditation certificate, with a mandatory risk assessment for sensitive data or continuous, large-scale transfers ([transfer regulation](https://dgp.sdaia.gov.sa/wps/portal/pdp/knowledgecenter/details/RegulationonPersonalDataTransferOutsidetheKingdom), Articles 4 and 7).
- **UAE health data:** Dubai Health Authority's [telehealth standard](https://dha.gov.ae/uploads/012023/Standards%20for%20Telehealth%20Services2023158613.pdf) (issue 4, effective 26 November 2025) prohibits storing, developing or transferring health data outside the UAE unless DHA approves (section 8.2.5(d)). That is an architectural constraint, not a contract clause.
- **Qatar:** the general law favours cross-border flows (Article 15), but "special nature" data, including health and children's data, needs permission from the regulator before processing (Article 16).

The usual failure is checking the primary database and stopping there. Backups, logs, crash reporting, analytics and AI APIs are all transfers. The [data residency guide](/en/blog/data-residency-gulf) walks through each one, and the [hosting comparison](/en/blog/hosting-comparison) lists which cloud regions are live in each country.

## Minors and AI: two areas to design for early

**Children's data.** Saudi Arabia handles minors through legal capacity: the guardian consents, and you must take reasonable steps to verify the guardianship (Implementing Regulation, Article 13). Qatar's Article 17 requires websites addressed to children to obtain explicit guardian consent and to delete a child's data on the guardian's request. An "I am over 18" checkbox satisfies neither.

**AI processing.** Sending user data to an external model is processing and, when the provider is abroad, a transfer. Before wiring up a provider, establish where inference runs, whether inputs are retained and whether they are used for training. [AI privacy risks](/en/blog/ai-privacy-risks) compares what the major providers state in their own terms.

## Where compliance work usually fails

1. **Production data in development environments,** long after anyone remembers the copy exists.
2. **Deletion that hides rather than deletes,** with the data surviving in backups and logs.
3. **Unmapped third parties:** analytics, crash reporting and push notifications nobody assessed.
4. **No consent records.** Without a timestamp and policy version, you cannot prove consent.
5. **A privacy policy describing someone else's product.**
6. **Assuming the vendor is accountable.** You are the controller; your agency is a processor.

## A pre-launch checklist

- [ ] Identified which jurisdiction applies, including any free-zone regime
- [ ] Mapped every category of personal data collected, and why
- [ ] Confirmed where primary data, **backups** and **logs** are stored
- [ ] Reviewed every third-party service as a possible transfer
- [ ] A documented lawful basis per processing activity
- [ ] Data subject rights available in the product
- [ ] Deletion that reaches backups and logs
- [ ] A privacy policy that reflects actual practice, in language your users understand
- [ ] A written breach procedure with named owners, built for a 72-hour deadline
- [ ] No production data in development or test environments
- [ ] A data processing agreement with every vendor
- [ ] Sector licensing identified **before** the architecture was settled

## Frequently asked questions

### Which Gulf data protection law applies to a company registered outside the Gulf?

The law of the country where your users are. Saudi Arabia's PDPL expressly covers processing by entities outside the Kingdom of data about people residing there, and the UAE federal law covers processing inside or outside the UAE. For Qatar, ask counsel, because its scope article does not address foreign companies directly.

### Do Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Qatar all require breach notification within 72 hours?

Not uniformly. Saudi Arabia requires notifying SDAIA within 72 hours of becoming aware. Qatar's regulator expects notice within 72 hours where the breach could cause damage. ADGM requires 72 hours where feasible, and DIFC requires notice as soon as practicable. For the UAE federal law we could not verify a fixed deadline, so ask a UAE adviser.

### Is DIFC or ADGM data protection the same as UAE federal law?

No. DIFC has its own Data Protection Law with a Commissioner, and ADGM has its own Data Protection Regulations 2021 with an Office of Data Protection. Which regime governs you depends on where your entity is incorporated, so check your trade licence before you design anything.

### Can I host Gulf user data in Europe or the US?

Often yes, on conditions. Saudi Arabia requires a route under SDAIA's transfer regulation, such as an adequacy listing or standard contractual clauses. Qatar's law stops controllers from restricting cross-border flows unless the processing breaches the law or risks serious damage. UAE health data is the hard exception: DHA prohibits storing it outside the UAE without approval.

### Who answers to the regulator if my development agency causes a breach?

You do. You are the controller and your agency is a processor. Put a data processing agreement in place and require the vendor to notify you within 24 hours of a suspected breach, so your own 72-hour window is not used up.

### When do I need a lawyer rather than this guide?

Before you finalise architecture if you are in health, finance or government, handle children's data, or operate from a free zone. For an ordinary commercial product, this guide plus a legal review of your privacy policy and processing agreements is usually proportionate.

## Where to start

Start with jurisdiction, then sector, then architecture, in that order. The expensive mistakes are all sequencing mistakes: choosing hosting before checking residency, building before licensing, launching before mapping what you collect. If you serve several Gulf markets, build once to the strictest rule that applies to you rather than maintaining three versions.
