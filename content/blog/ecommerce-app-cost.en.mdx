# How Much Does an E-Commerce Mobile App Cost?

A native e-commerce app costs between **$20,000 and $65,000** to build in 2026, and most of the store owners who ask for one do not need it yet.

That is an unusual way to open a pricing guide, but it is the honest starting point. An app is not a cheaper or better version of your store — it is a second storefront with its own build cost, its own release process, and its own ongoing maintenance. It earns its place when you already have repeat customers, and it wastes money when you are still trying to find them.

This guide covers what the money buys, when the numbers work, and how to tell which side of that line you are on.

---

## App or mobile website? Answer this first

Most stores reach for an app to solve a problem an app does not solve.

| Your situation | What you actually need |
|---|---|
| Traffic is low and you want more customers | Marketing and a faster mobile site — not an app |
| Mobile checkout is losing customers | Fix the checkout; an app inherits the same flow |
| Customers buy once and never return | Retention work first — an app nobody reopens is a dead cost |
| You have repeat buyers who order monthly | **An app makes sense** |
| You need push notifications to drive repeat sales | **An app makes sense** |
| You want to appear in the App Store for credibility | Real, but the weakest reason on this list |

The test is repeat purchase rate. An app is a retention tool, not an acquisition tool. Nobody discovers your store by browsing the App Store; they install your app because they already buy from you and want it to be faster next time.

If fewer than 20% of your customers order more than once, the same budget spent on the mobile site and on retention will return more.

For the store itself rather than the app, see [E-Commerce Store Cost in Saudi Arabia](/en/blog/ecommerce-development-cost-saudi).

## Cost table — 2026

| App type | Cost | What it includes | Timeline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Store wrapper (WebView) | $3,000 – $8,000 | Your existing site inside an app shell, push notifications | 3 – 6 weeks |
| Standard shopping app | $20,000 – $40,000 | Native browsing, cart, payments, order tracking, accounts | 3 – 5 months |
| Advanced app | $40,000 – $65,000 | Personalised recommendations, loyalty, multi-vendor, offline browsing | 5 – 8 months |
| Marketplace app | $65,000 – $120,000 | Multiple sellers, split payments, seller dashboards, disputes | 7 – 12 months |

These ranges sit inside the e-commerce band in our [mobile app development cost guide](/en/blog/mobile-app-development-cost), which covers how app pricing is built up in general.

**A note on the wrapper option.** A WebView app is cheap because it is mostly your website in a frame. It gets you push notifications and a home-screen icon, which are the two things most stores actually want. Apple rejects wrappers that add nothing beyond the website, so it needs at least some native behaviour to pass review. For a store testing whether customers will use an app at all, it is a reasonable $5,000 experiment before a $35,000 commitment.

## What drives the price

### Catalogue size and complexity

A hundred products with three variants each is a different build from twenty thousand products with size, colour, and warehouse-level stock. The cost is not in storing them — it is in search, filtering, and keeping stock accurate across the app, the website, and the warehouse at the same time.

Stores with configurable products — a sofa with fabric, size, and leg finish, where price depends on the combination — should expect the catalogue work alone to add $5,000 to $12,000.

### Payment methods

In the Gulf, supporting Mada, Apple Pay, and at least one BNPL provider is close to mandatory. Each integration is real work: sandbox testing, failure handling, refund flows, and reconciliation with your accounting.

Budget $1,500 to $4,000 per gateway. See [Payment Gateways in the Gulf](/en/blog/gulf-payment-gateways) for which ones matter and why.

### Integration with what you already run

This is the line item most quotes underestimate. Your app has to talk to your existing store, your inventory, your shipping providers, and your accounting system.

If you run Salla, Zid, Shopify, or WooCommerce, they expose APIs and the integration is predictable — $3,000 to $8,000. If you run a custom or older system with no API, someone has to build one first, and that can cost more than the app.

Ask any vendor quoting you: *what happens when stock changes on the website while a customer has the item in their app cart?* The answer tells you whether they have thought about synchronisation or are planning to discover it during the build.

### Shipping and tracking

Live rate calculation at checkout and real tracking updates require integrating each carrier separately. Aramex, SMSA, and the local post each have their own API and their own quirks. Budget $1,000 to $3,000 per carrier, and expect the second one to cost less than the first.

### Push notifications done properly

Push is the main reason to build an app, and it is usually treated as a checkbox.

Sending "20% off everything" to every user is how you train customers to disable notifications. Segmented, behaviour-triggered messages — abandoned cart, back in stock, order shipped — are what actually drive repeat orders, and they need event tracking and message logic behind them. Add $3,000 to $7,000 for that layer, and consider it the part of the budget that pays for the rest.

## The costs that continue after launch

An app is not a one-time purchase, and the second year surprises people who budgeted only for the build.

| Item | Annual cost |
|---|---|
| Maintenance and OS updates | 15 – 20% of build cost |
| Apple Developer Program | $99 |
| Google Play (one-time) | $25 |
| Push notification service | $0 – $1,200 |
| Backend hosting | $600 – $3,000 |
| New features | $5,000 – $15,000 |

The maintenance figure is not optional padding. iOS and Android each ship a major release every year, and an app that is not updated will eventually break on new devices and be removed from the store. See [In-House Maintenance or a Contract?](/en/blog/maintenance-inhouse-vs-contract) for how to structure that.

For a $35,000 app, expect roughly $12,000 in the second year. Over three years the true cost is close to double the build price — the same pattern covered in our [three-year ownership breakdown](/en/blog/mobile-app-development-cost).

## Does the math work? Calculate it yourself

Do this before you commission anything.

**Step 1 — What does the app need to earn?**
A $35,000 build plus $12,000 in year two is $47,000 over two years.

**Step 2 — What is your repeat customer worth?**
If a customer orders four times a year at 200 SAR with a 30% margin, that is 240 SAR of annual profit per active customer.

**Step 3 — How many app users would it take?**
47,000 SAR ÷ 240 SAR ≈ 196 active app customers over two years, if the app produced no additional orders at all.

**Step 4 — The real question.**
An app typically lifts order frequency among users who install it by 20–40%, because it removes friction and pushes reminders. So the honest question is not "will 196 people install it" but "do I have enough repeat customers that a 30% lift in their ordering covers $47,000?"

If you have 2,000 repeat customers, the answer is usually yes. If you have 150, it is usually no, and the same money spent on getting to 2,000 will serve you better.

## Cheaper paths worth considering first

**A progressive web app.** Installable from the browser, works offline, and sends push notifications on Android — and on iOS when the user adds it to the home screen. Costs $5,000 to $15,000 and needs no app store approval. The trade-off is discoverability and a weaker experience on iOS. See [Mobile App or Responsive Website?](/en/blog/app-vs-pwa).

**Your platform's own app.** Salla, Zid, and Shopify all offer branded apps on subscription. You get far less control and a monthly fee forever, but you are live in days rather than months. For a store with fewer than 500 monthly orders this is usually the right answer.

**Fix the mobile site first.** If your mobile checkout takes six steps and loads in five seconds, an app will not save you — it will reproduce the same funnel in a new place. Fixing that is cheaper and helps every visitor, not just the ones who install something.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does a simple e-commerce app cost?

A standard shopping app with browsing, cart, payment, and order tracking costs $20,000 to $40,000. A WebView wrapper around your existing store costs $3,000 to $8,000 and is a reasonable way to test demand first.

### Is an app better than a mobile website for selling?

For acquiring new customers, no — a fast mobile site reaches everyone, while an app reaches only people who already chose to install it. For retaining existing customers, yes: push notifications and a saved cart measurably raise repeat orders. The right sequence is site first, app once you have repeat buyers.

### How long does it take to build?

Three to five months for a standard app, plus one to three weeks for App Store and Google Play review. Plan for the review, especially the first submission, and read [App Store Requirements](/en/blog/app-store-requirements) before you build rather than after.

### Do I need separate iOS and Android apps?

No. Flutter and React Native produce both from one codebase and are the normal choice for e-commerce, cutting roughly 30–40% against two native builds. See [Flutter vs React Native](/en/blog/flutter-vs-react-native).

### Can I connect the app to my existing Salla or Zid store?

Yes. Both expose APIs, so the app reads the same products, stock, and orders as your store. Budget $3,000 to $8,000 for the integration and confirm before signing that stock synchronisation is included — it is the part most often left out.

### What ongoing costs should I expect?

Roughly 15–20% of the build cost annually for maintenance, plus $99 a year for Apple, hosting, and any new features. A $35,000 app typically costs around $12,000 in its second year.

### How many customers do I need for an app to be worth it?

As a rough test: enough repeat customers that a 30% lift in their order frequency covers roughly $47,000 over two years. For most stores that means a few thousand repeat buyers, not a few hundred.

## Conclusion

An e-commerce app costs $20,000 to $65,000, and the number matters less than the timing. The stores that get value from an app are the ones that already have customers coming back — the app makes returning easier and reminds them to. The stores that lose money on one are the ones that built it hoping to find customers inside it.

Run the calculation above with your own numbers before you talk to a vendor. If it does not clear comfortably, spend the money on your mobile site and your retention, and revisit the app in a year when the arithmetic has changed.
