# Software Company in Qatar: Selection Criteria and Pricing (2026)

Qatar's market is relatively small by population, but it is among the region's highest technology spenders. That creates a distinctive situation: **good budgets and less competition** — but it also means fewer local providers and pricing above the regional average.

This guide gives you realistic 2026 pricing, specific Qatari requirements that affect your budget, and practical evaluation criteria.

## What makes the Qatari market distinct?

**Government and quasi-government dominate.** A large share of Qatari software projects connect to government entities or major companies in energy and infrastructure. These carry higher documentation and compliance requirements and longer decision cycles, but better budgets.

**Bilingual is not optional.** Any platform serving a Qatari audience needs a correct Arabic version alongside English — and the government sector effectively mandates it.

**A narrower provider market.** There are fewer local development firms than in Dubai or Riyadh, which raises prices and makes engaging regional teams a common and sensible choice.

**A post-2022 economy.** The digital infrastructure built to host major events left a legacy of existing systems — meaning many projects today are **integration work** rather than building from scratch.

## Data protection: two regimes, not one

This is the point providers from outside Qatar most often get wrong, and it affects your project's cost.

**Qatar operates two parallel data protection regimes:**

**Inside the Qatar Financial Centre (QFC).** Entities registered within the centre fall under the **QFC Data Protection Regulations 2021**, which came into force in June 2022 and are overseen by the independent [Data Protection Office](https://www.qfc.qa/en/operating-with-qfc/data-protection) within the centre. These regulations are **designed to reflect the principles and rights of the European GDPR** — meaning they are more detailed and stricter, including expanded data subject rights.

**Outside the centre.** Entities registered in the state outside the centre's scope fall under Qatar's general personal data privacy protection law, a different framework with different requirements.

**Why this matters practically:** compliance cost and design requirements differ between the two regimes. A platform serving an entity inside the centre needs consent controls and access and deletion rights at a level close to GDPR — and that is designed in from the start, not added later.

> **Ask your provider directly:** "have you built for an entity inside the QFC before?" Anyone who does not know the difference between the two regimes will not design correct compliance for you. And always verify your regulatory position with your legal advisor, as frameworks change.

## Payment gateways and localisation

**Payments.** Local payment cards and Apple Pay are essential, alongside regional gateways operating in Qatar. Budget **$1,500 – $4,000 per gateway**, and verify your provider supports the gateways your audience actually uses rather than the ones they happen to know.

**Localisation.** Mirrored RTL layouts, appropriate Arabic typefaces, and professional translation — not machine translation. **$2,000 – $8,000**, rising considerably if added after the design is complete.

## Seven selection criteria

1. **A live portfolio** — working links on stores or the web, not slides.
2. **Qatari market experience** — have they built for a Qatari entity? Do they know the difference between the two data protection regimes?
3. **Who actually writes the code?** — outsourcing is common; concealing it is the problem.
4. **Genuine Arabic support** — ask to see an Arabic project they delivered, and judge the localisation quality yourself.
5. **Complete ownership** — code, domain, and store accounts in your name.
6. **Post-launch support** — warranty period and annual maintenance cost, agreed in writing.
7. **References you can speak to** — and ask: was the project delivered on schedule?

## Frequently asked questions

**How much does app development cost in Qatar?**

An MVP app ranges from $15,000 to $35,000 through a Doha firm, or $8,000 to $25,000 with a regional team. A mid-size business app reaches $80,000 locally. Qatari pricing sits roughly 15–30% above Saudi Arabia due to operating costs and fewer providers.

**Should I choose a Doha firm or a regional team?**

If your project is government-related or requires frequent in-person presence, a local firm suits better. For well-scoped projects, a regional team working in a nearby time zone delivers comparable quality at lower cost. What matters is that the provider understands localisation and Qatari data protection requirements.

**Which data protection regime applies in Qatar?**

Two regimes: entities inside the Qatar Financial Centre fall under the 2021 Data Protection Regulations designed to reflect European GDPR principles, while entities outside the centre fall under Qatar's general law. The difference directly affects your platform's design and compliance cost — so establish your regulatory position before development starts.

**Do I need an Arabic version?**

Yes in most cases, and effectively mandatory for government-facing projects. Professional localisation adds 25–40% and includes mirrored RTL layouts, not merely translated text. Machine translation is detected immediately and undermines credibility with an audience that reads Arabic fluently.

**How long does a project take?**

Brochure site: 2–5 weeks. Company site: 6–12 weeks. MVP app: 6–10 weeks. Mid-size app: 3–5 months. Enterprise platform: 9–18 months. Government projects run longer due to review and approval cycles, so plan for that in your schedule.

**What projects are most in demand in Qatar?**

Platforms connected to the government and quasi-government sector, event and hospitality management systems, and integration apps built on top of existing systems. Integration work is more common than building from scratch, given the established digital infrastructure.

Qatar's software market combines **good budgets with less competition** — but it imposes higher compliance requirements, particularly in distinguishing between the data protection regimes inside and outside the Qatar Financial Centre.
