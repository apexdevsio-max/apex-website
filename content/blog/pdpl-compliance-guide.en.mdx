# A Developer's Guide to Saudi PDPL Compliance

**If your product processes personal data of people residing in Saudi Arabia, the Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL) applies to you, wherever your company or servers are.** The law took effect on 14 September 2023, controllers had until 14 September 2024 to comply, and the Saudi Data and AI Authority (SDAIA) has enforced it since. In practice, compliance means building six things: purpose-specific consent or another documented lawful basis, a data-rights mechanism, a 72-hour breach procedure, controlled transfers abroad, a privacy policy, and security that matches the data.

> Written from an engineering perspective, not legal advice. Current as of November 2026.

This guide is for developers and product owners: what the law and its Implementing Regulation actually say, and what each requirement means in code. If you serve more than one Gulf market, start with the [Gulf regulatory compliance guide](/en/blog/gulf-compliance-guide), which compares the Saudi, UAE and Qatari regimes.

---

## Who does it apply to?

Article 2 of the [PDPL](https://dgp.sdaia.gov.sa/wps/portal/pdp/knowledgecenter/details/PDPL) covers processing of personal data in the Kingdom, including processing by an entity outside the Kingdom of personal data relating to individuals residing in the Kingdom. SDAIA's [guide for controllers and processors](https://dgp.sdaia.gov.sa/wps/portal/pdp/knowledgecenter/details/PDPLCP) confirms that such processing is covered even when it happens outside Saudi jurisdiction.

**Personal data** is broader than many teams assume: name, email, phone number, location, device identifiers and any data that, alone or combined, identifies a person. SDAIA's guide notes that fully anonymised data is no longer personal data, but pseudonymised (coded) data still is.

**Sensitive data** has a closed definition in Article 1 of the law. It covers:

- racial or ethnic origin
- religious, intellectual or political belief
- security and criminal data
- biometric data that identifies a person
- genetic data
- health data
- data indicating that one or both of a person's parents are unknown

Two categories teams often file under "sensitive" are not on that list. **Credit data** is defined separately in the law and has its own rules. **Data about minors** is not a sensitive category either; the rules for minors come through legal capacity and guardianship (see Special cases below).

## 1. Lawful basis: consent, and the other bases

Article 5 makes consent the default: personal data may not be processed, or its purpose changed, without the data subject's consent, except where the law provides otherwise. Consent can be withdrawn at any time.

**Article 6 lists the cases where consent is not required:**

1. Processing serves an actual interest of the data subject and contacting them is impossible or difficult.
2. Processing is required by another law or to perform a prior agreement the data subject is party to (in product terms: delivering the service the user signed up for).
3. The controller is a public entity and the processing is needed for security or judicial purposes.
4. Processing is necessary for the controller's legitimate interest, provided it does not prejudice the data subject's rights and interests and **the data is not sensitive**.

Legitimate interest comes with paperwork. Article 16 of the [Implementing Regulation](https://dgp.sdaia.gov.sa/wps/portal/pdp/knowledgecenter/details/PDPL2) requires a documented assessment before you rely on it, balancing your interest against the data subject's rights and reasonable expectations.

**What to build where consent is the basis:**

```
□ A separate checkbox per purpose (service / marketing / analytics)
□ Not pre-ticked
□ Plain-language text explaining each purpose
□ Withdrawal as easy as granting
□ A timestamped record: when, and against which policy version
```

**Marketing has its own article.** Article 25 requires the recipient's consent before you send promotional material to their email or postal address, and every message must offer a clear way to opt out. Article 26 allows processing for marketing only for non-sensitive data collected directly from a data subject who consented.

**Document a basis per processing activity.** "Consent for everything" is simpler to build, but if a user withdraws consent you did not need, you are left unable to perform something you were entitled to do anyway.

We build these consent and data-rights flows into the web platforms and apps we deliver; see our [web development service](/en/services/web-development) for how that fits into a project.

## 2. User rights: what your product must provide

Article 4 of the law gives data subjects five rights, and Article 3 of the Implementing Regulation sets the clock: **respond within 30 days**, extendable by up to another 30 days if the request needs unusual effort or several arrive at once, provided you tell the data subject in advance and give reasons.

| Right (Article 4) | What to build |
|---|---|
| **To be informed** of the legal basis and purpose of collection | Clear notices at the point of collection |
| **Access** to the data you hold | A "my data" page |
| **A copy** in a clear, readable format | An export function |
| **Correction**, completion or updating | Editable profile fields and a correction request path |
| **Destruction** of data no longer needed | Real deletion, subject to Article 18 |

**Building these into the product is far cheaper than handling them by hand** on every request.

**Deletion must delete.** Article 18 requires destroying data without delay once the purpose of collection ends, unless another law requires longer retention or the data relates to a pending court case. A `deleted_at` flag is not destruction, and data removed from the database but still sitting in logs and analytics exports has not been destroyed.

## 3. Breach notification within 72 hours

Article 20 of the law requires the controller to notify SDAIA when it learns of a leak, damage or unlawful access to personal data. Article 24 of the [Implementing Regulation](https://dgp.sdaia.gov.sa/wps/portal/pdp/knowledgecenter/details/PDPL2) sets the deadline: **within 72 hours of becoming aware of the incident**, where it may harm the data or the data subject. If you do not have every detail within 72 hours, you send what you have and provide the rest as soon as possible, with reasons for the delay. Affected data subjects must be told without undue delay, in plain and clear language, when the breach may cause them harm.

**What this means technically:**

- **Monitoring that detects a breach at all.** You cannot report within 72 hours a breach you discover two months later.
- **Logging detailed enough** to establish what leaked, when, and whose data.
- **A written procedure:** who is told internally, who decides, who contacts SDAIA.

Write the procedure before you need it.

## 4. Transferring data outside the Kingdom

Transfers are permitted, but conditionally. Article 29 of the law requires that a transfer does not prejudice national security or the Kingdom's vital interests, that the destination offers adequate protection, and that it is limited to the minimum data needed.

SDAIA's [Regulation on Personal Data Transfer Outside the Kingdom](https://dgp.sdaia.gov.sa/wps/portal/pdp/knowledgecenter/details/RegulationonPersonalDataTransferOutsidetheKingdom) turns that into routes:

- **Adequacy:** SDAIA publishes a list of countries and organisations with adequate protection and reviews it every four years (Article 3).
- **Appropriate safeguards** where there is no adequacy: standard contractual clauses, binding common rules, or an accreditation certificate (Article 4(1)).
- **Limited exemptions**, such as transfers that directly deliver a service to the data subject or occasional limited transfers, most of which do not apply to sensitive data (Article 4(2)).
- **A risk assessment** is mandatory when you rely on an exemption, and when you transfer sensitive data or transfer continuously or at scale (Article 7).

**What this means for a developer:** most SaaS products transfer continuously, and many vendors sit abroad. Every one of these is a transfer:

- **Hosting:** where the primary database actually lives.
- **Backups:** many managed services replicate to another region by default.
- **Logs and error tracking:** they usually contain personal data and often ship abroad.
- **Third-party services:** analytics, notifications, email, AI providers.

The [data residency guide](/en/blog/data-residency-gulf) walks through each of these; the [hosting comparison](/en/blog/hosting-comparison) lists which providers have live Saudi regions.

## 5. Development and testing environments

Neither the law nor the Implementing Regulation has a rule written specifically for test environments. That does not make a copy of production on a developer laptop safe. It is still processing of personal data, so the Article 19 duty to protect it with organisational, administrative and technical measures applies, as do the Regulation's data-minimisation rules. A forgotten database copy is also exactly the kind of incident that triggers the 72-hour clock.

**The alternative:**
- **Generated data** for testing.
- **Anonymisation** when you need realistic structure. Fully anonymised data falls outside the definition of personal data.
- **Masking** of identifying fields whenever any production data has to be copied.

> **Question for your lawyer:** if you must reproduce a production bug with real records, which measures (access logging, time-limited access, a written purpose) does counsel expect you to document?

## 6. Privacy policy

Article 12 requires every controller to adopt a privacy policy and make it available to data subjects before collecting their data. Article 13 lists what you must tell people at collection: the legal basis, the purpose, who is collecting, who will receive the data and whether it leaves the Kingdom, the consequences of not providing it, and their rights.

**On language:** SDAIA's [privacy policy guideline](https://dgp.sdaia.gov.sa/wps/portal/pdp/knowledgecenter/details/ElaborationandDevelopingPrivacyPolicyGuideline) does not name Arabic or English. It requires clear, non-misleading language that is easy to read and suited to the people whose data you process. For a product serving Saudi users that points to Arabic; adding English is a business choice for your audience, not a rule we could find in SDAIA's texts.

A policy copied from a foreign template is insufficient. It must describe what your product actually does, including every third-party service. Our [privacy policy guide](/en/blog/privacy-policy-guide) covers the content section by section.

## 7. Special cases

**Minors and others without full legal capacity.** Article 13 of the Implementing Regulation lets the legal guardian give consent and exercise rights on the data subject's behalf. You must take reasonable steps to verify the guardianship, the guardian's consent must not harm the data subject's interests, and the data subject must be able to exercise their own rights once they gain full capacity. A checkbox saying "I am over 18" verifies none of this. The [children's data guide](/en/blog/childrens-data-protection) covers verification methods.

**Health data.** Health data is sensitive. Article 23 of the law limits access to the fewest staff needed, and sector rules from health authorities apply on top. See our [clinic booking app guide](/en/blog/clinic-booking-app-cost).

**Data protection officer.** Article 32 of the Implementing Regulation requires a DPO if you are a public entity processing at large scale, if your core activities involve regular and systematic monitoring of individuals, or if your core activities involve processing sensitive data. The DPO can be an employee or an external contractor.

**Artificial intelligence.** Sending user data to an external language model is processing and, if the provider is abroad, a transfer. Check whether the provider retains inputs or trains on them. See [AI privacy risks](/en/blog/ai-privacy-risks).

## Technical checklist

- [ ] A documented lawful basis for each processing activity.
- [ ] Separate, unticked, withdrawable consent where consent is the basis.
- [ ] Timestamped consent records with the policy version.
- [ ] A legitimate-interest assessment on file wherever you rely on it.
- [ ] A page letting users view, export, correct and delete their data, with a 30-day response process.
- [ ] Deletion that covers backups, logs and analytics exports.
- [ ] A privacy policy in clear language for your audience that matches reality.
- [ ] A data map: where primary data, backups and logs live.
- [ ] Every third-party service reviewed as a possible transfer, with its route under the transfer regulation.
- [ ] No real production data in development environments.
- [ ] Encryption in transit and at rest, and access control by role.
- [ ] Monitoring that detects breaches, and a written 72-hour notification procedure.
- [ ] A record of processing activities, kept while processing continues and for five years after it ends (Implementing Regulation, Article 33).

## Who is accountable: you or your developer?

The law distinguishes the **controller**, which decides why and how personal data is processed, from the **processor**, which handles it on the controller's behalf. In a typical project **you are the controller and your development agency is a processor**, so regulatory accountability sits with you. You can put obligations on a vendor by contract, but not your own accountability to SDAIA.

**Before signing:**

- Put a **data processing agreement** in place: what the vendor may do with data, its security obligations, and what happens at contract end.
- Require **notice to you within 24 hours** of any suspected breach. Your own window to SDAIA is 72 hours, and a vendor that sits on the news for two days uses it up.
- Set **deletion obligations**, so no production data stays on a developer's machine after handover.
- Map **sub-processors**: the analytics, hosting and error-tracking services your vendor picks process your users' data too.

## What non-compliance costs

The penalties are in the law itself:

| Violation | Maximum penalty | Article |
|---|---|---|
| Disclosing or publishing sensitive data unlawfully, with intent to harm the data subject or for personal gain | Up to 2 years' imprisonment and/or a fine of up to SAR 3 million; the court may double the fine for a repeat offence | 35 |
| Any other violation of the law or its regulations by a private entity | A warning or a fine of up to SAR 5 million, doubled for a repeat violation | 36 |

Article 36 penalties are decided by committees formed by SDAIA, and the penalised party can appeal to the competent court.

**Enforcement is not theoretical.** On 16 January 2026 the [Saudi Press Agency reported](https://spa.gov.sa/en/N2489505) that SDAIA's committees had issued 48 decisions in 2025 confirming violations and imposing penalties under Article 36.

**The costs that usually arrive first** are less dramatic: rework when consent, deletion and audit logging have to be retrofitted into a shipped product; enterprise and government buyers asking for compliance evidence during procurement; and app store reviews that check your privacy disclosures (see [app store requirements](/en/blog/app-store-requirements)). And because notifying affected users is a legal duty, a breach also brings damage to your reputation.

**Scale the effort to the data.** A brochure site with a contact form is not in the same position as a health app. Do the data mapping first, because many teams find they hold more personal data than they assumed.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does the PDPL apply to a company with no office in Saudi Arabia?

Yes. Article 2 covers processing by an entity outside the Kingdom of personal data of individuals residing in the Kingdom. If you have users living in Saudi Arabia, your company's location does not exempt you.

### Is financial or children's data "sensitive data" under the PDPL?

No. The law's sensitive list covers racial or ethnic origin, belief, security and criminal data, biometric data that identifies a person, genetic data, health data and data indicating unknown parentage. Credit data is a separately defined category with its own rules, and minors are handled through legal capacity and guardian consent.

### Can I process data without consent under the PDPL?

Yes, in the cases Article 6 lists: to serve the data subject's interest when they cannot be reached, under another law or a prior agreement with the data subject, for a public entity's security or judicial needs, or for your legitimate interest when the data is not sensitive. Legitimate interest requires a documented assessment under Article 16 of the Implementing Regulation.

### What are the maximum PDPL penalties?

Up to SAR 5 million per violation under Article 36, doubled for repeat violations, and for unlawful disclosure of sensitive data with intent to harm or for gain, up to 2 years' imprisonment and a fine of up to SAR 3 million under Article 35. SDAIA's committees issued 48 penalty decisions in 2025.

### How fast must I answer a user's access or deletion request?

Within 30 days, under Article 3 of the Implementing Regulation. You may extend by up to another 30 days if the request needs unusual effort or several arrive together, but you must tell the user in advance and explain why.

### What does baseline PDPL engineering cost?

It is billed as engineering time. Ranges from our published rate card, as of November 2026: regional agency rates run $50–100 per hour (about SAR 200–400). How many hours depends on how many processing activities, vendors and data stores you have, which is why the data map comes first.
