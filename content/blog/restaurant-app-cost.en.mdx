# How Much Does a Restaurant App Cost?

Most restaurant apps cost **$15,000 to $45,000** (SAR 56,500–169,000 / AED 55,000–165,500 / QAR 54,500–164,000) and take 3 to 5 months, as of November 2026. That is the mid-complexity tier, and it is where ordering with in-app payment, loyalty or several branches lands. A single-restaurant order-ahead app is cheaper; running your own delivery drivers is a different, larger project.

Unlike most app categories, a restaurant app has a bill you can measure it against: the commission you already pay delivery aggregators. The worked example below shows when that arithmetic works and when it does not.

## Which tier your restaurant app falls into

| What you build | Tier | USD | SAR | AED | QAR | Timeline |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Order-ahead for one restaurant: menu, cart, pickup, order status, pay on collection | Simple | $5,000 – $15,000 | 19,000 – 56,500 | 18,500 – 55,000 | 18,000 – 54,500 | 6 – 10 weeks |
| Ordering with in-app payment, loyalty, several branches, POS integration | Mid-complexity | $15,000 – $45,000 | 56,500 – 169,000 | 55,000 – 165,500 | 54,500 – 164,000 | 3 – 5 months |
| Own delivery fleet: driver app, dispatch, live tracking, plus everything above | Advanced | $45,000 – $100,000 | 169,000 – 375,000 | 165,500 – 367,500 | 164,000 – 364,000 | 5 – 9 months |

Ranges from our published rate card, as of November 2026. The [mobile app development cost guide](/en/blog/mobile-app-development-cost) explains how the tiers are built.

The rate-card features that decide where you sit inside a tier:

| Feature | USD | SAR |
|---|---|---|
| In-app payments (cards, Apple Pay, mada through your gateway) | $3,000 – $7,000 | 11,500 – 26,500 |
| Each additional local payment gateway | $1,500 – $5,000 | 5,600 – 19,000 |
| Push notifications | $1,500 – $3,000 | 5,600 – 11,500 |

**A menu-only app is rarely worth building.** Nobody installs an app to read a menu they can see on your website, and Apple's [App Review Guideline 4.2](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/) expects an app to offer more than a repackaged website. If that is your budget, spend it on a fast mobile ordering page and your Google listing.

## What moves the cost inside the tier

### Menu structure

Menus look simple and are not. A burger with a choice of patty, three individually priced add-ons, a required side and an optional upgrade is a configuration problem, not a list item. Add branch-specific availability, time-based menus and items that sell out mid-service, and menu management becomes one of the larger pieces of work.

Insist on a menu tool your staff can update without calling the developer. If changing a price needs a support ticket, prices stop being updated and the app starts showing customers the wrong thing.

### POS integration

This is the difference between an app that works and one that creates chaos in the kitchen. Without integration, an app order arrives as a notification someone re-keys into the till; during a rush, that step gets skipped or mistyped. With integration, the order lands in the kitchen queue like any other.

Before you commission anything, ask your POS provider — Foodics, Marn, Square or whoever you use — whether it accepts orders from third-party apps through an API, and on what terms. If it does not, that becomes the deciding constraint on the whole project. POS integration is one of the things that puts an ordering app in the mid tier rather than the simple one.

### Delivery: pickup, partner or your own fleet

- **Pickup only.** The cheapest version and a sound first release. No driver logistics.
- **Third-party fulfilment.** A logistics partner moves the food while you keep the customer relationship and the order. The integration stays in the mid tier.
- **Your own drivers.** Driver assignment, live tracking and a driver app move the project into the advanced tier, $45,000–$100,000, and add an operational load most single-site restaurants underestimate.

[Delivery App Development Cost](/en/blog/delivery-app-cost) covers the driver side in depth.

### Loyalty that changes behaviour

A points system is easy to build and easy to ignore. What changes behaviour is targeting: a free coffee for someone who came three times last month and has not returned this month, or a weekday offer for customers who only visit at weekends. That needs order history, segmentation and scheduled campaigns, which is why loyalty sits in the mid tier with push notifications behind it.

## The commission arithmetic

Run this with your own figures. Every input below is an assumption chosen to show the method, not a market benchmark: take your order count and average ticket from your POS, and the commission rate from your aggregator contract.

**Inputs for the example:** 3,000 aggregator delivery orders a month, SAR 70 average order, 25% commission, 2.5% card processing on direct orders, SAR 12 per order if you deliver yourself, a $30,000 (SAR 112,500) mid-tier app, and maintenance at 15–20% of the build a year (SAR 1,400–1,875 a month).

**What you pay today:** 3,000 × SAR 70 × 25% = **SAR 52,500 a month** in commission.

**Suppose 600 of those orders a month move to your app.** That number is yours to estimate; it depends entirely on how hard you promote the app.

- Commission saved: 600 × 70 × 25% = SAR 10,500
- Card processing on those orders: 600 × 70 × 2.5% = SAR 1,050

**If those orders become pickup orders:** SAR 10,500 − 1,050 = SAR 9,450 a month, or about SAR 7,600–8,000 after maintenance. The app pays back in roughly **14–15 months**.

**If you deliver them yourself:** subtract 600 × SAR 12 = SAR 7,200 for delivery, leaving SAR 2,250 a month, or SAR 375–850 after maintenance. On commission savings alone that takes more than ten years to recover, and an own-fleet app sits in the advanced tier, not at $30,000.

**The break-even volume.** Each order moved to pickup saves SAR 70 × (25% − 2.5%) = SAR 15.75. To recover SAR 112,500 in 24 months (SAR 4,690 a month) plus SAR 1,875 a month of maintenance, about **420 orders a month** have to move to direct pickup.

The point is not the example's answer. It is that pickup and partner delivery are where the commission case is strongest, and an own fleet needs a reason beyond commission.

## The problem nobody prices: installs

Building the app is the easier half. Getting customers to install it is the harder half, and it is where restaurant apps quietly fail. Your customers already have an aggregator app with every restaurant in the city, their saved address and their card. Yours has one restaurant.

What works:

- **A concrete install incentive at the till:** a first-order discount printed on the receipt and mentioned by staff.
- **Loyalty that only exists in the app:** points that cannot be earned on the aggregator.
- **QR codes on tables and packaging,** where the customer is already thinking about your food.
- **Direct prices below aggregator prices,** which you can afford because no commission is taken.

Plan and budget this before you approve a build. An app nobody installs saves no commission at all.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a pickup-only ordering app cheaper than one with delivery?

Yes, by a full tier at the extremes. Order-ahead for one restaurant with payment on collection is a simple app at $5,000–$15,000. Adding in-app payment and loyalty moves it to $15,000–$45,000, and running your own drivers with live tracking is an advanced build at $45,000–$100,000.

### How many orders must leave the aggregator for an app to pay back?

In this guide's example — SAR 70 orders, 25% commission, 2.5% card fees and a $30,000 app — about 420 orders a month moving to direct pickup recovers the build and maintenance in two years. Put your own contract rate and ticket size into the same formula.

### Does connecting to Foodics or another POS change the price?

It is one of the features that puts an ordering app in the mid tier rather than the simple one. The bigger question is whether your POS accepts orders from third-party apps through an API at all; confirm that with the provider before you scope anything else.

### Why does running my own drivers cost so much more?

Because it adds a second app for drivers, dispatch logic and real-time GPS tracking ($4,000–$8,000 on its own), which together move the project into the advanced tier. In the worked example, own-fleet delivery also cuts the monthly saving from SAR 9,450 to SAR 2,250.

### What should I offer customers to install the restaurant's own app?

Something the aggregator cannot match: app-only loyalty points, direct prices below aggregator prices, and a first-order discount promoted at the till and on packaging.

### Should I keep listing on delivery aggregators after launching an app?

Yes. Aggregators bring you new customers; your app keeps the ones you already have at a lower cost per order. Use the aggregator for discovery and the app for repeat orders.
