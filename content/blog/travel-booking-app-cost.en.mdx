# How Much Does a Hotel and Travel Booking App Cost?

**A booking app for a single hotel or tour operator selling its own inventory is a mid-complexity build at $15,000–$45,000 (SAR 56,500–169,000 / AED 55,000–165,500 / QAR 54,500–164,000). A multi-supplier booking platform — an aggregator pulling rooms or trips from suppliers you do not control — is an advanced platform at $45,000–$100,000 (SAR 169,000–375,000 / AED 165,500–367,500 / QAR 164,000–364,000).**

Ranges from our published rate card, as of November 2026. Booking apps are integration projects wearing an app's clothes. The screens — search, results, a room, a payment — are the quick part. What consumes the budget is connecting to the systems that hold the inventory and making sure two people never book the last room at the same moment. Our [mobile app development service](/en/services/mobile-apps) scopes booking projects with that integration work priced in from the start.

## Which kind of booking app are you building?

The biggest cost driver is where your inventory comes from.

| Model | What it means | Tier | USD | SAR | AED | QAR | Timeline |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single property | One hotel, its own rooms and calendar | Mid-complexity app | $15,000 – $45,000 | 56,500 – 169,000 | 55,000 – 165,500 | 54,500 – 164,000 | 3 – 5 months |
| Tours and activities | Fixed departures, capacity per slot, guides | Mid-complexity app | $15,000 – $45,000 | 56,500 – 169,000 | 55,000 – 165,500 | 54,500 – 164,000 | 3 – 5 months |
| Small chain | Several properties, one brand, one back office | Mid-complexity app, or advanced if each property runs a different system | $15,000 – $45,000 or $45,000 – $100,000 | 56,500 – 169,000 or 169,000 – 375,000 | 55,000 – 165,500 or 165,500 – 367,500 | 54,500 – 164,000 or 164,000 – 364,000 | 3 – 9 months |
| Aggregator / OTA | Inventory from suppliers you do not control | Advanced app or platform | $45,000 – $100,000 | 169,000 – 375,000 | 165,500 – 367,500 | 164,000 – 364,000 | 5 – 9 months |

A single hotel building its own app is a manageable project. An aggregator competing with Booking.com is a different business, and the app is the cheapest part of it; supplier contracts sit outside any build price.

**Be honest about which one you are.** Many briefs describe an aggregator and budget for a single property.

## Where the money actually goes

### The availability engine

This is what separates a booking app from a catalogue, and it is where inexperienced quotes go wrong.

Availability is not a number you store. It is a calculation over a date range that accounts for minimum and maximum stay, closed-to-arrival days, seasonal rates, and rooms held in baskets that have not checked out yet. A search for "3 nights, 2 adults, next weekend" runs that calculation across every room type before it can show a price.

Then there is the problem that defines the category: **two customers reaching the last room at the same time.** Handling it correctly means holding inventory during checkout, releasing it if payment fails, and never confirming a booking the property cannot honour. Getting it wrong produces overbookings, which cost money and reputation in a way a broken feature never does.

The engine is the core of the mid tier for a single property. A quote that treats it as a minor line item is evidence the vendor has not built one before.

### Channel manager and PMS integration

A hotel that sells on Booking.com, Expedia and its own app cannot maintain three calendars by hand. It runs a channel manager that keeps them in sync, and your app has to speak to it.

This is per-system work, and it is not only technical. [SiteMinder's developer hub](https://developer.siteminder.com/get-started/partner-lifecycle/integration-process), for example, takes partners through an integration process to apply, build, certify and go live. Each system has its own API, rate structures and way of expressing restrictions. Integrating with one channel manager fits inside a single-property build; integrating with several, or with each property's own system, is what moves a chain or aggregator into the advanced tier.

For flights or multi-supplier hotel inventory you need a global distribution system or a supplier aggregator. That brings contracts, certification and per-transaction fees, and it is a commercial process as much as a technical one. Start those conversations before you build; they can take months and they decide what the app can sell.

### Payments and the money flow

Travel payments are not retail payments. Decide before development starts:

- Do you charge at booking, at check-in, or both?
- Who holds the money between booking and stay — you or the property?
- What happens on cancellation, and who absorbs the gateway fee?
- Do you offer instalments for higher-value bookings?

If you collect payment and pay properties later, you are handling other people's money, which can raise payment-licensing questions in Gulf markets. Settle your position with your gateway and a legal adviser before you design the flow. Our guide to [payment gateways in the Gulf](/en/blog/gulf-payment-gateways) covers the options by country.

### Cancellation and modification

Every travel product has cancellation rules, and they are more intricate than they look: free until 48 hours before, then one night, then non-refundable, varying by rate plan, season and property. Encode them in the booking engine as part of the build. Skipping them means your support team processes cancellations by hand, which works until volume arrives.

## What most quotes leave out

**Content.** Photographs, descriptions, amenities and policies for every property. For one hotel this is a weekend; for fifty properties it is a project, and stale content is a common complaint in travel app reviews.

**Multiple currencies and languages.** Regional travel apps need Arabic and English at minimum, and a Gulf traveller booking abroad expects prices in SAR, AED or QAR. Currency conversion needs a rate source and a decision about who absorbs fluctuation. See our [Arabic localization guide](/en/blog/arabic-localization-guide) for the language side.

**Hijri dates.** A Gulf-facing app that cannot display or accept Hijri dates frustrates a real share of its users, especially around Ramadan, Hajj and Umrah. Covered in [Working with Hijri Dates](/en/blog/hijri-dates-guide).

**Seasonal load.** Traffic is not flat. Hajj, Umrah, Eid and school holidays produce spikes far above the baseline, and infrastructure sized for an ordinary Tuesday will fail on the morning Umrah slots open. Budget for load testing and headroom.

| Item | How it is billed |
|---|---|
| Maintenance | 15–20% of the build cost per year |
| Channel manager subscription | By the channel manager, per property |
| Payment processing | A percentage per transaction, set by your gateway contract |

Payment processing is the item that grows with success rather than sitting flat. As an illustration only: if your gateway charged 2.5% on 500,000 USD of annual bookings, that would be 12,500 USD a year, which belongs in the business model rather than the IT budget.

## Is it worth building?

For a **single property**, the honest comparison is against the commission you already pay. Take an illustrative hotel with 2,000 bookings a year at an average of 800 SAR that pays 15% commission to booking sites: 240,000 SAR a year. Shifting a quarter of those to a direct app saves 60,000 SAR a year, against a mid-tier build of SAR 56,500–169,000 plus 15–20% annual maintenance. That pays back in roughly one to three years, faster if the app brings guests back more often.

There is a caveat worth stating plainly: **guests who book direct usually already know you.** An app will not bring new guests the way a booking-site listing does; it converts existing demand more cheaply. If your occupancy depends on discovery through booking sites, the app complements that channel rather than replacing it.

For an **aggregator**, the app is not the question. Supplier contracts, inventory access and the cost of acquiring customers are the business, and they will dwarf the development budget. Build the commercial side first.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does a direct-booking app for one hotel cost?

It is a mid-complexity app on our rate card: $15,000–$45,000 (SAR 56,500–169,000 / AED 55,000–165,500 / QAR 54,500–164,000), built in 3–5 months, including the availability engine, booking and cancellation rules, and an admin dashboard.

### Why is a booking app pricier than a shopping app of the same size?

Because availability is calculated, not stored, and two people must never book the same room. Holds, releases, restrictions and sync with the systems that own the inventory make up most of the work, and none of it is visible on screen.

### Do I need a channel manager integration from day one?

If you also sell on Booking.com or Expedia, yes; otherwise you keep calendars by hand and will eventually overbook. If the app is your only sales channel, you can start without one and add it later. Channel managers run their own partner certification, so allow time for it.

### When does a travel app move from the mid tier to the advanced tier?

When you sell inventory you do not control: several suppliers, several property systems, or flights. That makes it an advanced platform at $45,000–$100,000 (SAR 169,000–375,000 / AED 165,500–367,500 / QAR 164,000–364,000), before supplier contracts.

### Can I launch without a GDS connection?

Yes, if you sell your own rooms or tours. A GDS or supplier aggregator is needed to sell flights or third-party hotel inventory, and it brings contracts, certification and per-transaction fees. Most single-property apps never need one.

### Should a hotel keep listing on booking sites after launching its app?

Usually yes. Booking sites bring discovery your app cannot; the app lowers your cost for guests who already know you. Treat it as a way to reduce your blended commission, not eliminate it.
