# Choosing an AI Model for an Arabic Assistant: Decision Criteria

**As of November 2026, the best-documented choices for an Arabic assistant are the hosted frontier models — Anthropic's Claude, OpenAI's GPT models, and Google's Gemini — and, when data must stay on your own servers, the Arabic-first open models Jais 2 and Falcon-H1-Arabic. Of the hosted providers, only Anthropic publishes an Arabic score for its current models: Claude Opus 5.5 reaches 99.3% of its English score on a multilingual benchmark.**

**The verdict:** shortlist one mid-tier hosted model from each of the three providers (or the two open models if you must self-host), then run 30–50 of your own customer conversations through each and pick on the results. Published scores tell you a model handles Arabic; only your own test tells you it handles *your* customers' Arabic.

Model names change every few months, so the table below is dated and the testing method after it is the part that lasts. If you would rather have the evaluation run for you, it is part of our [AI solutions](/en/services/ai-solutions) work.

## How to choose: three routes

**Route 1 — a hosted assistant on ordinary commercial data (most projects).** Shortlist a mid-tier model from each provider — for example Claude Sonnet 5.5, GPT-6.2 Sol, and Gemini 3.8 Flash — plus one smaller, cheaper tier to see whether it is good enough. Run the test below and pick the cheapest model that passes.

**Route 2 — data that must stay on your own infrastructure.** Shortlist the Arabic-first open models: Jais 2 (Apache 2.0, explicitly covers dialects and code-switching) and Falcon-H1-Arabic (several sizes, long context). Add Llama 4 if you want a general-purpose model with official Arabic support. Budget for GPU hosting and the people to run it — [AI for business](/en/blog/ai-for-business-guide) explains why self-hosting usually costs more than teams expect.

**Route 3 — you are not sure you need a model.** Check the table in the next section first. Many Arabic customer-service needs are met without one.

## Six selection criteria

### 1. Arabic quality — and dialect specifically

Published scores come from Modern Standard Arabic benchmarks. Your customers may not write in it.

**Test specifically:**
- Formally written Modern Standard Arabic.
- **Your actual audience's dialect** — Gulf, Egyptian, Levantine.
- **Arabizi** (Arabic in Latin script: "3ayez a3rf").
- Mixing Arabic and English within one sentence.
- Common spelling variations (hamza forms, taa marbuta).

Anthropic's documentation also advises submitting text in its native script rather than transliteration for best results — a hint that Arabizi is where you should expect weaker answers from any model.

### 2. Context window size

The context window is how much text a model can read in a single request. It ranges widely — 4,096 tokens for ALLaM 7B, 8,192 for Jais 2, up to 256K for the larger Falcon-H1-Arabic models.

**In practice:** most customer service applications do not need enormous windows. If you pass an entire document on every request, the problem is in the design rather than the model — use a retrieval step that extracts only the relevant passages, as described in [RAG and AI knowledge bases](/en/blog/rag-knowledge-base).

### 3. Cost per conversation

Hosted models charge per million tokens, and prices vary between providers and versions while changing continuously.

**Calculate like this:**
`conversation cost ≈ input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price`

**An important point for Arabic:** tokenizers differ, and Arabic can take several times more tokens than English for the same meaning. Two models at the same headline price can cost different amounts for the same Arabic conversation. [What AI costs to run](/en/blog/llm-running-costs) has the arithmetic.

**A practical test:** take 20 real conversations from your logs, run them through each candidate, and calculate actual cost. That figure beats any theoretical estimate.

### 4. Response latency

In a live conversation, the difference between two seconds and six is a genuine experience difference.

**Smaller models are faster, cheaper, and less capable.** Many applications run well on a smaller tier — but check its Arabic specifically, since that is where small tiers lose most.

**A useful pattern:** a small fast model for common questions, escalating to a stronger one when the question is complex.

### 5. Privacy and data residency

**This may settle the decision before any technical criterion.**

If your assistant processes Saudi customer data, the Personal Data Protection Law applies, including its rules on transferring data outside the Kingdom — see [PDPL compliance](/en/blog/pdpl-compliance-guide).

**Ask every provider:**
- In which country or cloud region are our requests processed?
- Are our inputs used to train models? Anthropic's [privacy center](https://privacy.claude.com/en/articles/7996868-is-my-data-used-for-model-training) and OpenAI's [data controls](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/your-data) both state that API inputs are not used for training by default.
- How long are logs kept?
- Can we run it in a regional data centre, or self-host it?

**Do not pass sensitive personal data to any model before verifying these points** with your legal advisor.

### 6. Stability and version changes

Models are updated and retired. Ask whether you can pin a specific version and how much notice you get before one is discontinued.

**A design rule:** build your system so **the model can be swapped by changing one setting**, not by rewriting. This protects you from price and capability changes, and makes it cheap to re-run the test when a new model ships.

## How to test for yourself

This is the section that stays valid however the model names change.

**1. Gather 30–50 real cases** from your customer conversations, covering easy, hard, and ambiguous — in the dialects your customers actually use.

**2. Write the correct answer for each** — this is your evaluation reference.

**3. Run them through every candidate model** with exactly the same instructions (prompt), stating the response language explicitly in the system prompt.

**4. Score on three criteria:** accuracy (is the information correct?), linguistic quality (is the Arabic sound and in the right register?), and grounding (did it stick to your sources or invent?).

**5. Measure actual cost and latency** for each model on the same sample.

**The result:** a decision built on your own data, not a general comparison that may not resemble your case at all.

---

## Reducing cost without reducing quality

- **Shorten the system prompt.** It is sent with every request, so each extra word is paid for thousands of times.
- **Pass only relevant excerpts**, not entire documents.
- **Cache answers to repeated questions** — no need to call the model for the same question a hundred times.
- **Use a smaller model for simple tasks** and escalate only when needed.
- **Cap answer length** — long answers cost more and are rarely better.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best AI model for Arabic in 2026?

There is no single winner, but there is a clear shortlist. For a hosted assistant, test a mid-tier model from Anthropic (Claude Sonnet 5.5), OpenAI (GPT-6.2 Sol) and Google (Gemini 3.8 Flash); Anthropic publishes Arabic scores of 96.6–99.6% of English across its current models. If you must self-host, test Jais 2 and Falcon-H1-Arabic. Pick the one that scores best on 30–50 of your own conversations.

### Is there a Saudi or Emirati Arabic language model?

Yes. SDAIA's National Center for AI publishes ALLaM-7B-Instruct-preview, an Arabic–English model with open weights. From the UAE, MBZUAI, Inception and Cerebras publish Jais 2 (70B, Apache 2.0), and TII publishes Falcon-H1-Arabic in 3B, 7B and 34B sizes. All three can be self-hosted, which matters when data must stay in the country.

### Do the big hosted models understand Gulf dialect?

None of Anthropic, OpenAI or Google publishes a Gulf-dialect score; their Arabic figures come from Modern Standard Arabic benchmarks. Jais 2 and Falcon-H1-Arabic explicitly cite dialect data in their documentation. Either way, include Gulf-dialect and Arabizi messages from your own logs in the test set — that is the only reliable answer.

### Will my data be used to train the model?

On the API, Anthropic and OpenAI both state that inputs are not used for training by default. Consumer chat apps carry different terms, and processing location and log retention still need checking against the Personal Data Protection Law with your legal advisor before sensitive data is sent.

### What if the model gives wrong information?

This is the primary risk. Three practices reduce it: **restricting the model to answer only from your approved content**, setting a confidence threshold below which it hands off to a human, and reviewing conversation logs regularly. Human handoff is a necessity, not an optional extra.

### Can I switch models later?

Yes, if you design for it — make the model a swappable setting rather than something embedded in code. You will usually need to retune the prompt and retest, but the architecture holds. With new models shipping every few months, this is the most important design decision in the project.

### Do I need a custom model trained on my data?

Rarely. Most cases are solved by a general model plus search over your documents (RAG) — cheaper, faster, and easier to update. Custom training earns its cost only with large, highly specialised datasets and a budget that justifies it.

## Where to start this week

Pull 30 real conversations from your support logs, keep the dialect and the typos, and write the answer you would want for each. With that set in hand, comparing Claude, GPT, Gemini or an open Arabic model takes days rather than a procurement cycle — and the result is still valid when the next model ships, because you simply run it again.
