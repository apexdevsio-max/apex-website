# Mobile App Testing: What Is Actually Worth Testing

**Test business logic first, with fast unit tests; cover the critical paths (sign-up, payment, booking) with a few integration tests; and add a regression test for every bug that reaches production. Don't automate purely visual UI.** That is close to what [Flutter's testing documentation](https://docs.flutter.dev/testing/overview) recommends: "many unit and widget tests, tracked by code coverage, plus enough integration tests to cover all the important use cases."

Testing tends to be discussed in extremes: one team writes no tests at all, another chases 100% coverage and spends more time on tests than on the product. Some tests repay their cost many times over and others are maintenance burden with no return. This guide separates the two, then covers manual checks, beta distribution and post-launch monitoring with the actual limits Apple and Google publish.

---

## The testing pyramid, with Flutter's numbers

The familiar rule is many unit tests, fewer integration tests, and fewer still end-to-end UI tests. Flutter's documentation splits the middle into widget tests and states the trade-offs directly:

| Type | What it tests | Confidence | Maintenance cost | Speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Unit** | A single function, method or class | Low | Low | Quick |
| **Widget** (component) | One widget: layout, interaction, children | Higher | Higher | Quick |
| **Integration** (E2E) | The whole app or a large part, on a device or emulator | Highest | **Highest** | Slow |

**Why this order?** End-to-end tests give the most confidence, but they are **brittle**: they break with minor design changes and take real time to maintain. Unit tests run in milliseconds and only break when the logic genuinely changes.

One limit worth knowing: Flutter's `integration_test` package cannot interact with native platform UI such as permission dialogs or notifications. Those flows need a third-party tool or a manual check, which is one more reason the manual checklist below still matters.

React Native follows the same pyramid with different tools; the principles in this article apply to both. If you are still choosing a framework, see [Flutter vs React Native](/en/blog/flutter-vs-react-native).

## What is worth testing, by priority

### 1. Business logic: the highest return

Calculations, validation, business rules, data transformation.

**Why?** Because this is the logic whose **errors cost real money**: a wrong price calculation, a discount applied twice, an incorrect due date.

It is also **the easiest to test**: pure functions with clear inputs and outputs, no UI and no network.

> **A real example.** In [Tafawwoq](/en/portfolio/tafawwoq-educational-app), the Arabic education app we built in Flutter with Clean Architecture and BLoC, the quizzes run on a timer with automatic grading. Separating the domain layer from the UI is what made the quiz and scoring logic testable in isolation from the interface. In a product that calculates students' grades, that is the logic you most want covered. The structure behind it is explained in our [Clean Architecture guide](/en/blog/clean-architecture-flutter).

### 2. Critical paths

The paths whose breakage stops your business: registration, purchase, payment, booking.

**Test the whole path** end to end, even manually at first, then automate the one or two that would cost the most if they broke.

### 3. What broke before

**Every bug that reaches production deserves a test that stops it coming back.** These are the highest-return tests because they're built on a real failure, not a guess.

### 4. Edge cases

The empty list, a dropped connection, extremely long text, a negative number, a future date.

**Developers tend to forget these, and users tend to find them.**

Building testing into the plan from the start is part of our [mobile app development service](/en/services/mobile-apps); it is far cheaper than retrofitting it after launch.

## What isn't worth automating

- **Purely visual UI** such as colours and spacing. A person reviewing the screen is faster and more accurate.
- **Generated code.** Don't test what you didn't write.
- **Thin wrappers**, such as a function that only passes a parameter through.
- **Screens that change weekly.** Testing a screen under redesign is burden with no return.
- **Third-party libraries.** Test your integration with them, not the libraries themselves.

## Manual testing: still indispensable

Automation doesn't notice that a button is out of thumb reach, that Arabic text is truncated, or that an animation looks janky.

### A manual checklist before every release

**Functionality:**
- [ ] Critical paths work end to end.
- [ ] Sign-in, sign-out, and password reset.
- [ ] Payment (with a test account).
- [ ] Permission dialogs (camera, location, notifications), which automated Flutter integration tests can't drive.

**Devices:**
- [ ] A **mid-range** Android device, not the newest.
- [ ] An iOS device.
- [ ] One small screen and one large.

**Network:**
- [ ] A slow connection: performance and waiting states.
- [ ] A drop mid-operation: does it recover gracefully?
- [ ] Reconnection: does it resume?

**Language and direction:**
- [ ] The app fully in Arabic. See our [RTL guide](/en/blog/flutter-rtl-arabic-guide).
- [ ] Switching languages doesn't break the layout.
- [ ] Long text isn't truncated or overflowing.

**Edge cases:**
- [ ] Empty lists show a useful message.
- [ ] Very large datasets degrade gracefully.
- [ ] Denied permissions: the app explains and continues.

## Testing on real devices

**An emulator isn't enough.** It doesn't show actual performance on a slow processor, battery drain, real camera and GPS behaviour, or how the app interacts with other apps. Flutter's own [performance documentation](https://docs.flutter.dev/perf/ui-performance) says emulators and simulators are "generally not indicative" of release behaviour.

**The practical minimum:** one mid-range Android device and one iOS device. **The mid-range Android matters more than the newest one**, because performance problems surface there first.

## Testing before launch: beta tracks and their limits

**Beta releases.** Distribute to a limited group before general launch. The two stores publish different limits:

| Channel | Who can test | Limit | Review |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Apple TestFlight](https://developer.apple.com/testflight/), internal | Team members with an App Store Connect role | Up to 100 | No |
| Apple TestFlight, external | Anyone you invite | Up to 10,000 | First build must be approved by App Review |
| [Google Play](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9845334), internal track | Testers you list | Up to 100 per app | Quick distribution for QA |
| Google Play, closed and open tracks | A wider group, or anyone on Play (open) | Larger | Stages before production |

**One requirement that catches new publishers:** Google Play personal developer accounts created after 13 November 2023 must run a closed test with [at least 12 testers opted in continuously for 14 days](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14151465) before they can apply for production access. Plan those two weeks into the launch date. Our [app store requirements guide](/en/blog/app-store-requirements) covers the rest of the submission checklist.

**Real users find what your team doesn't**, because they use the app the way they want, not the way you designed it.

**Upgrade testing.** If you have existing users, test upgrading from the previous version, not only a clean install. **Data migration is a recurring source of bugs** that only existing users see.

**Load testing**, if your app expects a peak such as a season, a campaign, or a launch. See our [hosting guide](/en/blog/hosting-comparison).

## After launch: monitoring is continuous testing

**Crash reporting is mandatory.** Without it, you only hear about crashes from the users who bother to complain.

On Android, Google Play also watches for you. Its [Android vitals](https://developer.android.com/topic/performance/vitals) treat these as bad behaviour, measured over the last 28 days:

| Core vital | Overall threshold | Per phone model |
|---|---|---|
| User-perceived crash rate | 1.09% | 8% |
| User-perceived ANR (app not responding) rate | 0.47% | 8% |

Above these thresholds, Google says Play may reduce your app's visibility and may show users a warning on the store listing. So crash monitoring affects acquisition as well as quality.

**Also watch:**
- **Which screens cause crashes**, not just the overall rate.
- **Unexpected errors** on the server.
- **Screens users abandon**, which may be slow or confusing.

**No pre-launch test gives you this data**, because it comes from real usage on real devices and networks.

## How many tests are enough?

There is no magic coverage percentage. **Better practical criteria:**

- **Critical business logic is covered**: calculations and business rules.
- **Every past production bug has a test** that stops it returning.
- **Critical paths are tested**, even manually with a fixed checklist.
- **Tests run fast.** If they take ten minutes, the team will skip them.

**A sign you're overdoing it:** if a minor change breaks twenty tests, your tests are coupled to implementation, not behaviour. That's burden, not protection.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does writing tests make an app more expensive to build?

Up front, yes: tests are code and take time to write. Unit tests for business logic are the cheapest kind; broad UI automation is the most expensive to write and maintain, which is why it should be limited to the critical paths. The saving shows up later, when you can change a year-old codebase without fear of breaking payments or grades.

### Should an MVP have automated tests?

Test the critical business logic and the core path only. Broad automation is overkill at a stage where everything may change. See our [MVP guide](/en/blog/mvp-vs-full-product).

### What do manual testers catch that automated tests miss?

Automated tests confirm that what worked still works, and they are cheap to repeat. Manual testing finds what nobody anticipated: a poor experience, truncated Arabic text, a confusing flow, a permission dialog that blocks the user. **You need both**: automation for regression protection, manual review for quality.

### Do I need a dedicated QA tester?

For large projects and regulated sectors, it's worth it. For smaller projects, a disciplined developer with a fixed written checklist covers most of the need. **The written checklist matters more than who runs it.**

### How do I get my app onto testers' phones before launch?

Use TestFlight on iOS (up to 100 internal and 10,000 external testers, with App Review for the first external build) and Google Play's testing tracks on Android (up to 100 testers on the internal track). If you publish from a new personal Play account, budget 14 days for the mandatory closed test with at least 12 testers.

### What crash rate is acceptable on Android?

Google Play's bad-behaviour threshold for user-perceived crash rate is 1.09% averaged across devices and 8% on any single phone model, measured over 28 days. Treat those as ceilings, not targets: crossing them can reduce your app's visibility on Play.

## Where to start

- **Unit-test the business logic** first: the highest return and the easiest to write.
- **Write a regression test for every production bug.**
- **Leave visual UI to manual review.**
- **Test on a real mid-range device**, because an emulator isn't enough.
- **Plan the beta**, including Play's 14-day closed test for new accounts.
- **Monitor crashes after launch**, against Play's published thresholds.
