# Outsourcing or Building an In-House Team?

The instinct once a product matters is to bring development in-house. It feels like control, it feels cheaper than agency invoices, and it feels like what a serious company does.

**The short answer: build in-house when software is your product and you need continuous development. Outsource when you need a product built, a capability you lack, or capacity you cannot justify permanently. Most growing companies end up with a hybrid, and that is usually correct rather than a compromise.**

This guide is about the ongoing model. For choosing an external partner, see [how to choose a development company](/en/blog/choosing-development-company).

## The cost comparison nobody does properly

Comparing a developer salary to an agency rate is the standard mistake. The salary is roughly 60–70% of what an employee actually costs.

**A realistic annual cost for one mid-level developer:**

| Item | Indicative annual cost |
|---|---|
| Salary | $30,000 – $60,000 |
| Benefits, insurance, end-of-service | +15 – 25% |
| Recruitment (agency fee or time) | $3,000 – $10,000 (year one) |
| Equipment and software licences | $2,000 – $4,000 |
| Office space and overhead | $3,000 – $8,000 |
| Management time | Real, and rarely counted |
| **Effective total** | **$45,000 – $90,000** |

**But cost per developer is the wrong unit anyway.** A product needs design, mobile, backend, QA, and DevOps. One developer is not a team — they are a bottleneck with a salary.

**A minimum viable in-house team** is roughly three to four people: $150,000–$300,000 annually, fixed, before anyone has shipped anything.

**The comparison that matters** is not "developer vs agency rate." It is **fixed annual capacity against variable project cost.** An in-house team costs the same in a quiet quarter. An external team costs nothing when you are not building.

## The question that actually decides it

Not "which is cheaper" — that depends entirely on utilisation. The real question:

> **Will you have continuous development work for the next two years?**

**If yes** — a genuine roadmap, ongoing iteration, a product that is your business — in-house wins. You are paying for capacity you will use, institutional knowledge compounds, and response time collapses from days to minutes.

**If no** — you are building one product, then maintaining it — outsourcing wins clearly. Hiring a team to build one app and then keeping them employed to maintain it is paying full price for partial utilisation.

**The honest test:** write down what your in-house team would do in month eight, after launch. If the answer is thin, you are hiring for a project, not a function.

## What each model actually gives you

**In-house strengths:**
- **Institutional knowledge.** After a year, the team knows why every decision was made.
- **Response time.** A question answered in the corridor rather than a ticket.
- **Alignment.** They live with the consequences of their choices.
- **Confidentiality.** Genuinely simpler when the domain is sensitive.

**In-house costs beyond salary:**
- **Hiring takes months** and you may hire wrong. A bad hire costs more than any agency invoice.
- **You need someone who can evaluate developers.** A non-technical founder hiring their first engineer is a known failure pattern.
- **Skill gaps are structural.** Your Flutter developer cannot do your DevOps, and a small team has no bench.
- **Retention.** Developers leave. Losing your only backend engineer stops the product.

**Outsourcing strengths:**
- **Speed to start.** Weeks, not months.
- **Breadth on demand.** Design, mobile, backend, QA without five hires.
- **Variable cost.** You stop paying when you stop building.
- **They have done it before.** Pattern recognition across many projects is real value.

**Outsourcing costs beyond the rate:**
- **Context lives outside your company.** Mitigated by documentation, never eliminated.
- **Priority competition.** You are one of several clients.
- **Handover risk.** If the relationship ends badly and the code is undocumented, you have a problem. This is what [contract terms](/en/blog/app-development-contract) exist to prevent.

## The hybrid most companies land on

Presented as a compromise, it is usually the right answer for a growing product company.

**The common shape:**
- **In-house:** a technical lead or product owner who holds the vision, makes architectural decisions, and owns the relationship
- **Outsourced:** the build capacity — design, development, QA — scaled to what is actually needed

**Why it works:** you get institutional knowledge and decision-making authority in-house at the cost of one senior salary, while build capacity stays variable. The internal lead can evaluate the external team's work, which solves the hardest problem in outsourcing — knowing whether you are getting good work.

**The variant that fails:** outsourcing with *nobody* technical internally. Then you cannot assess quality, cannot make architectural calls, and are entirely dependent on the vendor's judgement. If you cannot hire a technical lead, this is the strongest argument for choosing a partner carefully and insisting on the ownership terms that let you leave.

## Regional notes

**The Gulf hiring market is competitive and expensive.** Salaries for experienced developers in Saudi Arabia and the UAE are well above regional averages, and hiring timelines are long. This shifts the calculation toward outsourcing more than it would in a lower-cost market.

**Regional outsourcing has a real advantage** over distant offshore: overlapping working hours, Arabic-native communication, and cultural context on the product itself. A team three time zones away turns a one-day clarification into a three-day round trip.

**Localisation experience is not interchangeable with general skill.** If your product is Arabic-first, a team without RTL experience will learn on your project — see [Arabic localisation](/en/blog/arabic-localization-guide) for the scope of what they would be learning.

**Regulated sectors change the answer.** Health, fintech, and government work carry licensing knowledge that takes years to build in-house. Outsourcing to a team that has navigated it before is usually faster and cheaper than acquiring it. See [Gulf regulatory compliance](/en/blog/gulf-compliance-guide).

## A decision framework

**1. Is software your product, or does it support your product?**
Your product → in-house is likely correct eventually. Supporting → outsource.

**2. Do you have two years of continuous development work?**
No → outsource. The utilisation maths does not work.

**3. Can you evaluate a developer's work?**
No → hire one technical person first, whatever else you decide.

**4. Do you need a capability you lack?**
Mobile, AI, compliance-heavy domains → outsource rather than hire into a gap you cannot assess.

**5. Is your budget fixed or variable?**
Uncertain revenue favours variable cost. In-house is a fixed commitment regardless of how the business performs.

**6. How fast do you need to start?**
Under three months → outsource. Hiring takes longer than that.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is outsourcing cheaper than hiring in-house developers?

It depends entirely on utilisation. A minimum viable in-house team of three to four people costs $150,000 to $300,000 annually as a fixed commitment, regardless of workload. Outsourcing is variable — you stop paying when you stop building. For continuous multi-year development, in-house usually wins; for building one product then maintaining it, outsourcing wins clearly.

### What does an in-house developer actually cost?

Roughly 1.5 times salary once you include benefits, end-of-service, recruitment, equipment, office overhead, and management time — commonly $45,000 to $90,000 annually for one mid-level developer in the Gulf. But cost per developer is the wrong unit, since a product needs design, mobile, backend, and QA.

### When should I build an in-house team?

When software is your product rather than a supporting function, and you have genuine continuous development work for the next two years. The honest test is writing down what the team would do in month eight after launch. If that answer is thin, you are hiring for a project rather than a function.

### What is the hybrid model?

One technical lead or product owner in-house who holds the vision, makes architectural decisions, and evaluates work quality, with build capacity outsourced and scaled to need. It gives you institutional knowledge for the cost of one senior salary while keeping development cost variable, and it solves the hardest problem in outsourcing — knowing whether the work is good.

### Can I outsource without anyone technical in my company?

You can, but it is the weakest position: you cannot assess quality, cannot make architectural decisions, and depend entirely on the vendor's judgement. If hiring a technical lead is not possible, compensate by choosing the partner carefully and insisting on contract terms that guarantee code ownership and a clean exit.

### Is offshore outsourcing worth the cost saving?

Often less than it appears. A team several time zones away turns a one-day clarification into a three-day round trip, and that friction consumes the saving. Regional outsourcing with overlapping hours and shared language usually delivers better total value than the lowest hourly rate available.

### How long does it take to hire a development team?

Months, typically three to six for a small team, and longer in competitive Gulf markets. If you need to start within three months, outsourcing is the only realistic option — and that timing constraint decides the question more often than cost does.

## Conclusion

**Utilisation decides this, not hourly rates.** In-house is fixed capacity; outsourcing is variable cost. The question is whether you will actually use the capacity you are committing to.

**Write down what your team does in month eight.** It is the most honest test available, and a thin answer means you are hiring for a project rather than a function.

**And if you outsource, get one technical person in-house.** Not to write code, but to evaluate it. That single hire converts the weakest version of outsourcing into the strongest.
