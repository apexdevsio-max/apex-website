# Redesigning a Website: Cost, Timing, and How Not to Lose Your Traffic

**A full website redesign costs what a new site of the same size costs: $1,500–$5,000 for a landing page or small brochure site, $8,000–$25,000 for a mid-size company website and $25,000–$80,000 for a custom web application.** In Gulf currencies the company-website tier is about SAR 30,000–94,000, AED 29,500–92,000 or QAR 29,000–91,000. Ranges from our published rate card, as of November 2026.

The most expensive part, though, is rarely the design. It is the traffic you lose when the new site launches with different URLs and nobody maps the redirects. This guide covers when a redesign is justified, what it costs, and the migration work that separates a redesign from a self-inflicted outage.

---

## First: do you need a redesign or a repair?

Most redesign requests start from one specific frustration that a redesign is an expensive way to fix.

| The complaint | What it usually needs |
|---|---|
| "It looks dated" | Sometimes real — but define what "dated" means before spending |
| "It's slow" | Performance work, not a redesign — see [website speed optimisation](/en/blog/website-speed-optimization) |
| "It doesn't work on phones" | Responsive fixes, unless the codebase makes that impractical |
| "We can't update it ourselves" | A CMS migration, which may not require a new design |
| "It doesn't rank" | Content and SEO work — a redesign alone changes nothing |
| "It doesn't convert" | Analytics first; you cannot fix what you have not measured |
| "Our business changed" | **A genuine redesign reason** |
| "It's unmaintainable" | **A genuine rebuild reason** |

The two genuine reasons are the last two: the site describes a business you no longer are, or the codebase has reached the point where every change risks breaking something else. If your reason is one of the first six, get a specific diagnosis before committing to a rebuild.

## The part that actually decides the outcome

### Redirects

When a page moves from `/services/web-design` to `/what-we-do/web`, every link pointing at the old address — from Google's index, from other sites, from bookmarks — now leads nowhere.

A permanent redirect (HTTP 301 or 308) tells search engines the page has moved for good. Google [says](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/301-redirects) its indexing pipeline uses a permanent redirect "as a signal that the redirect target should be canonical", and recommends a permanent server-side redirect whenever a URL changes. Without one, the new page starts with no history.

The work is not glamorous:

1. Export every URL on the current site before touching anything.
2. Map each one to its equivalent on the new site.
3. Decide what to do with pages that have no equivalent.
4. Implement the redirects on the server.
5. Test them after launch, starting with your most valuable pages.

Google's [site-move guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes) asks you to keep the redirects "for as long as possible, generally at least 1 year." Budget for the mapping explicitly and confirm it appears in your vendor's quote; if it does not, ask why before signing.

### Pages with no equivalent

Some pages will not survive the redesign. For each one, decide deliberately:

- **Redirect to the closest relevant page** if the topic is still covered.
- **Redirect to the parent section** if the specific page is gone but the category remains.
- **Return a 404 or 410** if the content is genuinely retired. Google [treats all 4xx codes except 429 the same way](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/http-network-errors) and drops the URL from its index, so either works.

What you should not do is redirect everything to the homepage. Google's site-move guide says plainly: "Don't redirect many old URLs to one irrelevant single URL destination, such as the home page of the new site", because it confuses users and may be treated as a soft 404.

### Keep what already works

Before redesigning, find out which pages bring you traffic and enquiries today. Search Console's performance report and your analytics will show it. Usually a small number of pages produce most of the value, and they are often not the ones anyone in the company thinks about.

Those pages should change the least. Redesigning a page that ranks and converts, purely because it does not match the new visual language, is how a redesign loses money. Fit the new design around them rather than through them.

## What most quotes leave out

**Content migration.** Moving text and images is manual work when the platforms differ. On a large site it is a real line item, and it is often quietly assumed to be the client's job. Clarify who does it.

**The staging review.** You need to see the new site fully populated before it goes live, and time to check it. Build a review period into the timeline rather than discovering problems on launch day.

**Analytics continuity.** If tracking is reinstalled incorrectly, you lose the ability to tell whether the redesign helped. Confirm tracking is carried across and verified before launch.

**Post-launch monitoring.** Google says a small to medium-sized site "can take a few weeks for most pages to move." Someone needs to watch crawl errors and broken redirects in Search Console during that window.

## A launch sequence that protects your traffic

1. **Record your baseline.** Current traffic, rankings and conversion rate. Without it you cannot tell whether the redesign worked.
2. **Export every URL** and build the redirect map before development finishes.
3. **Review on staging** with the real content in place.
4. **Launch, then test redirects immediately** — a sample of at least twenty, including your most valuable pages.
5. **Submit the updated sitemap** in Search Console so recrawling starts promptly, as Google's site-move guide recommends.
6. **Watch for four weeks.** Google says to "expect temporary fluctuation in site ranking during the move." A drop that is still deepening after three or four weeks means something is wrong and needs investigating rather than waiting out.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does a website redesign cost in 2026?

On our rate card, as of November 2026, a full redesign is priced like a new build: $1,500–$5,000 for a landing page or small brochure site, $8,000–$25,000 (about SAR 30,000–94,000) for a mid-size company website and $25,000–$80,000 for a custom web application. A visual refresh that keeps the same pages costs less than the build tier for your size.

### Will a redesign hurt my search rankings?

It can, badly, if URLs change without permanent redirects. Done properly — a full redirect map, an updated sitemap and monitoring — Google says to expect temporary fluctuation, and most pages of a small to medium site move within a few weeks. The risk sits in the migration, not the design.

### How long should I keep the old-URL redirects after relaunch?

Google recommends keeping them as long as possible, generally at least one year, and suggests considering keeping them indefinitely. Removing them early sends every old link and bookmark to an error page.

### Can I redesign without changing my URLs?

Yes, and it is the safest option. A visual refresh that keeps your existing structure carries almost no search risk. If your URLs are reasonable, keeping them is usually the better decision even when the design changes completely.

### What should I do with pages I am removing?

Redirect each to the closest relevant page, or to its parent section if no direct equivalent exists. Return a 404 or 410 for content genuinely retired; Google treats them the same. Do not redirect everything to the homepage — Google warns this may be treated as a soft 404.

### How do I know if the redesign worked?

Compare against the baseline you recorded before launch: traffic, rankings and conversion rate. Give it at least eight weeks, since early fluctuation is normal. Without a baseline you cannot answer the question at all.
