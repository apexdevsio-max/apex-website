# Redesigning a Website: Cost, Timing, and How Not to Lose Your Traffic

A website redesign costs between **$4,000 and $35,000**, and the most expensive thing about it is rarely the design.

It is the traffic you lose when the new site launches with different URLs and nobody set up redirects. Sites that ranked well for years disappear from search results in a fortnight, and recovering takes months — if it happens at all.

This guide covers when a redesign is actually justified, what it costs, and the migration work that separates a redesign from a self-inflicted outage.

---

## First: do you need a redesign or a repair?

Most redesign requests are triggered by one specific frustration that a redesign is an expensive way to fix.

| The complaint | What it usually needs |
|---|---|
| "It looks dated" | Sometimes real — but define what "dated" means before spending |
| "It's slow" | Performance work, not a redesign — often a fraction of the cost |
| "It doesn't work on phones" | Responsive fixes, unless the codebase makes that impractical |
| "We can't update it ourselves" | A CMS migration, which may not require a new design |
| "It doesn't rank" | Content and SEO work — a redesign alone changes nothing |
| "It doesn't convert" | Analytics first; you cannot fix what you have not measured |
| "Our business changed" | **A genuine redesign reason** |
| "It's unmaintainable" | **A genuine rebuild reason** |

The two genuine reasons are the last two: the site describes a business you no longer are, or the codebase has reached the point where every change risks breaking something else.

If your reason is one of the first six, get a specific diagnosis before committing to a full rebuild. Slow sites can often be fixed for a tenth of a redesign budget — our [hosting comparison](/en/blog/hosting-comparison) covers one common cause.

These sit alongside new-build pricing in our [website design cost guide](/en/blog/website-design-cost), which covers what determines the figure in each case.

**The line worth understanding:** a visual refresh keeps your URLs, your content, and your rankings. Anything that changes the URL structure introduces migration risk, and that risk is what makes the middle two rows more expensive than they look.

## The part that actually decides the outcome

### Redirects

When a page moves from `/services/web-design` to `/what-we-do/web`, every link pointing at the old address — from Google's index, from other sites, from anyone's bookmarks — now leads nowhere.

A 301 redirect tells search engines the page moved permanently and transfers most of its accumulated authority to the new location. Without it, you are launching a new site with no history.

The work is not glamorous:

1. Export every URL on the current site before touching anything
2. Map each one to its equivalent on the new site
3. Decide what to do with pages that have no equivalent
4. Implement the redirects
5. Test every single one after launch

For a fifty-page site this is a day's work. Skipping it can cost a year of rankings. Budget for it explicitly and confirm it appears in your vendor's quote — if it does not, ask why before signing.

### Pages with no equivalent

Some pages will not survive the redesign. For each, decide deliberately:

- **Redirect to the closest relevant page** if the topic is still covered
- **Redirect to the parent section** if the specific page is gone but the category remains
- **Return a 410** if the content is genuinely retired and should leave the index

What you should not do is redirect everything to the homepage. Search engines treat a mass redirect to the homepage as a soft 404 and pass none of the authority along, so it delivers the worst of both options.

### Keep what already works

Before redesigning, find out which pages currently bring you traffic and enquiries. In most cases a small number of pages produce most of the value, and they are frequently not the ones anyone in the company thinks about.

Those pages should change the least. Redesigning a page that ranks well and converts, purely because it does not match the new visual language, is how a redesign loses money. Match the new design around them rather than through them.

## What most quotes leave out

**Content migration.** Moving text and images is manual work when the platforms differ. For a large site this is a real line item, and it is often quietly assumed to be the client's job. Clarify who does it.

**The staging review.** You need to see the new site fully populated before it goes live, and you need time to check it. Build a review period into the timeline rather than discovering problems on launch day.

**Analytics continuity.** If your tracking is reinstalled incorrectly, you lose the ability to tell whether the redesign helped. Confirm tracking is carried across and verified before launch, not after.

**Post-launch monitoring.** Search engines take weeks to fully recrawl a changed site. Someone needs to watch for crawl errors and broken redirects during that window. Budget a few hours a week for the first month.

## A launch sequence that protects your traffic

1. **Record your baseline.** Current traffic, current rankings, current conversion rate. Without this you cannot tell whether the redesign worked.
2. **Export every URL** and build the redirect map before development finishes.
3. **Review on staging** with the real content in place.
4. **Launch, then immediately test redirects** — a sample of at least twenty, including your most valuable pages.
5. **Submit the updated sitemap** to Search Console so recrawling starts promptly.
6. **Watch for four weeks.** Some ranking fluctuation is normal after a change. A sustained drop after three weeks means something is wrong and needs investigating rather than waiting out.

Expect a modest dip in the first two weeks even on a well-executed migration. Search engines need to re-evaluate a changed site, and that is temporary. What is not normal is a drop that deepens rather than recovers.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does a website redesign cost?

A visual refresh that keeps your existing structure costs $4,000 to $9,000. A full redesign with new design and existing content runs $9,000 to $20,000. Restructuring the site as well brings it to $15,000 to $28,000, and a rebuild on a new platform starts around $20,000.

### Will a redesign hurt my search rankings?

It can, badly, if URLs change without 301 redirects. Done properly — full redirect mapping, sitemap resubmission, and monitoring — a redesign usually causes a brief dip that recovers within weeks. The risk is entirely in the migration, not the design.

### How often should a website be redesigned?

There is no fixed interval. Redesign when the site no longer represents your business or the codebase has become unmaintainable — not on a schedule. Sites that are maintained and updated can run for many years without a full redesign.

### Can I redesign without changing my URLs?

Yes, and it is the safest option. A visual refresh that keeps your existing structure carries almost no search risk. If your URLs are reasonable, keeping them is usually the better decision even when the design changes completely.

### How long does a redesign take?

Three to five weeks for a visual refresh, six to ten for a full redesign, and three to five months for a rebuild on a new platform. Content migration and the review period are the phases most often underestimated.

### What should I do with pages I am removing?

Redirect them to the closest relevant page, or to their parent section if no direct equivalent exists. Return a 410 only for content genuinely retired. Do not redirect everything to the homepage — search engines treat that as a soft 404 and pass no authority.

### How do I know if the redesign worked?

Compare against the baseline you recorded before launch: traffic, rankings, and conversion rate. Give it at least eight weeks, since early fluctuation is normal. If you did not record a baseline, you will not be able to answer this question at all.

## Conclusion

A website redesign costs $4,000 to $35,000, and the design is the part least likely to go wrong. What goes wrong is the migration: URLs change, redirects are skipped, and a site that took years to rank starts again from nothing.

Before approving a quote, ask for two things in writing: the redirect plan, and which existing pages will keep their URLs. A vendor who has done this before will have clear answers ready. One who has not will treat both as details to sort out later — and later is after your traffic has gone.
