# Hosting Your Site: Vercel, AWS, or Local Hosting?

**Host most company websites and Next.js stores on a deployment platform such as Vercel; move to AWS, Azure or Google Cloud when you need managed databases, background jobs and variable load; and choose an in-country cloud region when your sector requires data to stay in the Gulf.** Vercel's Hobby plan is free and its Pro plan costs $20 per developer seat a month plus usage beyond the included allowance. For Saudi residency in particular, note that as of November 2026 AWS has announced but not opened a Saudi region, Azure's Dammam region is in preview, and Google Cloud's Dammam region is sold through a local reseller.

This guide compares the providers. The legal question of *when* your data must stay in-country — and the backups, logs and third-party services that quietly break residency — is covered in our separate [data residency guide for the Gulf](/en/blog/data-residency-gulf).

## The four options

| Option | Suits | How you pay |
|---|---|---|
| **Shared hosting** | Small WordPress sites | A low fixed monthly fee |
| **Deployment platforms** (Vercel, Netlify) | Modern sites, Next.js | Free tier, then per seat plus usage (Vercel Pro: $20 per developer seat a month) |
| **Major cloud** (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud) | Complex platforms, variable load | Pay per use, per service |
| **In-country or regional cloud** | Data residency requirements | Pay per use or by contract, sometimes through a local reseller |

If you are planning a new site or platform and want the hosting chosen as part of the build, our [web development team](/en/services/web-development) includes it in the architecture rather than leaving it to launch week.

## Deployment platforms — the default for most sites

**[Vercel](https://vercel.com/)**, **[Netlify](https://www.netlify.com/)** and similar services are built to deploy modern websites with minimal operational effort: connect a code repository, every update deploys automatically, and a global content delivery network is included.

**What the plans include:** Vercel's [Hobby plan](https://vercel.com/docs/plans/hobby) is free with monthly allowances (for example the first 100 GB of fast data transfer and the first 1,000,000 function invocations). Pro adds team collaboration, email support and spend management at $20 per developer seat a month, with viewer seats free and usage beyond the included credit billed on demand.

**Advantages:** no server management, automatic deployment, a global CDN, automatic SSL certificates.

**Drawbacks:** cost grows with traffic and function usage, less control over infrastructure, and you do not choose the country where every request is served — which may rule it out under strict residency rules.

**Suits:** brochure sites, blogs, mid-size stores, any Next.js project.

---

## Major cloud — for complex platforms

**AWS**, **Azure** and **Google Cloud** give you complete control and practically unlimited scaling.

**Advantages:** full control, integrated managed services (databases, storage, queues), and **regions in the Gulf** that let you choose where data is stored.

**Drawbacks:** genuine operational complexity that needs expertise, bills that are hard to predict without controls, and higher running cost for small sites.

**Suits:** platforms with variable load, enterprise systems, projects that need a specific data region.

> **A cost warning:** cloud bills escalate quietly — a forgotten service running for months with no traffic. Set budget alerts on day one.

## Gulf cloud regions, verified November 2026

Global comparisons jump from Vercel to AWS and stop there. For Gulf projects, the question is which provider actually has a live region in your country — and on what terms.

| Country | AWS | Azure | Google Cloud |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Saudi Arabia** | Announced, not yet in AWS's region list | Saudi Arabia East (Dammam) — **preview** | `me-central2` (Dammam) — Saudi-billed customers buy through the reseller CNTXT |
| **UAE** | `me-central-1` Middle East (UAE) | UAE North (Dubai); UAE Central (Abu Dhabi) is access-restricted, mainly for disaster recovery | No region listed |
| **Qatar** | No region listed | Qatar Central (Doha) | `me-central1` (Doha) |
| **Bahrain** | `me-south-1` Middle East (Bahrain) | No region listed | No region listed |

Sources: [AWS Regions](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/global-infrastructure/latest/regions/aws-regions.html), [Azure regions list](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/reliability/regions-list), [Google Cloud regions and zones](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/regions-zones), [Google Cloud Dammam region access](https://docs.cloud.google.com/docs/dammam-region-access).

**What this means in practice:**
- **Saudi Arabia** is the market where "region available" needs the most checking. AWS's Saudi regions are announced, not live; Azure's Dammam region is in preview (Microsoft asks you to contact sales for service availability); Google Cloud's Dammam region is live but, for customers with a Saudi billing address, is bought and supported through CNTXT rather than directly from Google.
- **The UAE** has the most mature choice: AWS and Azure both run general-purpose regions there.
- **Qatar** has two hyperscalers in Doha (Azure and Google Cloud).
- AWS requires you to opt in to its Bahrain and UAE regions before use, and Azure's UAE Central needs an access request.

**What you trade with a regional option.** A newer or reseller-run region often has a smaller service catalogue at launch — Google says new regions start with core services and add others within six months — and support may come through a partner. Budget for that friction; it is usually smaller than the cost of getting residency wrong.

> **Worth knowing:** "has a region in the country" and "your data stays in the country" are not the same statement. Backups, logs and support access can cross borders by default. Our [data residency guide](/en/blog/data-residency-gulf) walks through each of these.

## What to ask any provider

Six questions that expose the real differences before you sign:

**1. Where, geographically, is the data stored — including backups and logs?** The most important question if you are in a regulated sector.

**2. What uptime is guaranteed, and what is the compensation if it is missed?**

**3. Who runs backups, and has a restore been tested?** A backup nobody has tried restoring is not a backup.

**4. What happens during a sudden traffic spike?** Automatic scaling or a stop? And what is the cost ceiling?

**5. What does data transfer (bandwidth) cost?** The line that surprises most people on a cloud bill.

**6. How do I leave?** Confirm you own your data and can export it.

> **A practical rule:** test the provider's support before you sign, not after. Send a technical question and measure the response time and quality — that is what you will depend on during an outage.

## What hosting does not include

Most hosting-cost surprises come from assuming these are bundled. They usually are not.

| Item | How it is usually charged | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Domain name | Annual registration | Country domains such as `.sa` and `.qa` have their own registration rules |
| SSL certificate | Often free | [Let's Encrypt](https://letsencrypt.org/about/) issues trusted certificates at no cost; deployment platforms add them automatically |
| Email hosting | Per user, per month | **Separate from web hosting** — the most common omission |
| Backups | Sometimes included, often not | Ask explicitly, and ask where they are stored |
| CDN | Included with deployment platforms | Usually extra on raw cloud |
| Monitoring and error tracking | Free tiers, then per usage | Check where error reports are processed if you have residency rules |
| Bandwidth overage | Usage-based | The line that produces shock bills |

**Email deserves its own warning.** Hosting your website and hosting `info@yourcompany.com` are separate purchases. Teams routinely discover this after launch — and a company still using a free webmail address as its public contact reads as unestablished to business buyers, which is a commercial cost, not only a technical one.

## Migrating hosting without breaking search rankings

Changing hosting is where sites quietly lose search visibility. The move itself is rarely the problem; the handling around it is. Google's own guidance for [a hosting move that keeps the same URLs](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-no-url-changes) covers the essentials.

**Before the move:**
1. Lower your DNS TTL well ahead — Google suggests at least a week — so the cutover propagates quickly.
2. Record current rankings and traffic; you cannot detect a regression without a baseline.
3. Copy every redirect rule. Lost redirects are the most common cause of post-migration ranking loss.
4. Verify the new environment serves the same URLs, including trailing-slash behaviour.

**During:**
5. Keep the old server running until its traffic reaches zero. Turning it off early guarantees downtime for users still resolving to the old address.
6. Confirm SSL is valid on the new host *before* switching DNS.

**After:**
7. Check `robots.txt` immediately. A staging rule that disallows all crawling, copied to production, stops search engines crawling the whole site.
8. Verify canonical tags still point at the production domain, and that your Search Console verification file or tag survived the move.
9. Watch server logs on both old and new hosts and index coverage in Search Console for at least two weeks.
10. Compare Core Web Vitals against your baseline.

> **The one that ends projects:** a staging `robots.txt` reaching production. It is a two-line file, it is easy to overlook, and lost index coverage takes time to recover. Check it first, not last.

## Performance and geography

The distance between server and visitor affects speed: a server in Europe serving a visitor in Riyadh is slower than one in the region.

**The gap narrows considerably** with a content delivery network that caches copies close to the visitor. For a content site, a good CDN handles most of the problem.

**When does geography still matter?** For interactive platforms that hit the database on every request. A CDN does not help there, and a region close to your users makes a noticeable difference.

## The decision framework

**1. Is your project in a regulated sector (health, finance, government)?**
Yes → start with the residency rules in our [data residency guide](/en/blog/data-residency-gulf), then choose from the regions in the table above that satisfy them.

**2. Do you need complex infrastructure (multiple databases, background processing, variable load)?**
Yes → major cloud.

**3. Is your site content or ordinary commerce?**
Yes → a modern deployment platform. This is the correct default for most projects.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need hosting inside Saudi Arabia for a company website?

For an ordinary brochure or commerce site, usually not: global hosting with a CDN serves Saudi visitors well. If you handle health, financial or government data, check the residency rules first — our [data residency guide](/en/blog/data-residency-gulf) explains what each Gulf country requires.

### What will Vercel cost for a typical company site?

Vercel's Hobby plan is free with monthly allowances, and Pro costs $20 per developer seat a month, with viewer seats free and usage beyond the included credit billed on demand. A company site with two developers on Pro starts at $40 a month before any overage.

### Is cheap shared hosting enough for a small business site?

For a small WordPress site with limited traffic, yes. But it degrades quickly with growth, and its security depends on the provider and on the other sites sharing the server. For any serious commercial project, the step up to a deployment platform or a small cloud instance is a justified cost.

### How do I avoid a surprise cloud bill?

Set budget alerts on day one, review active services monthly and delete unused ones, and never enable auto-scaling without a ceiling. Most shock bills come from a forgotten service running idle rather than genuine growth.

### Can I start on Vercel and move to AWS later?

Yes, though the effort varies. A static site moves within hours; a platform with a database and integrations needs planning and possibly a maintenance window. **If residency requirements are plausible in your future, choose a provider with a suitable region from the start** — moving later costs far more.

### Which cloud providers have a live region in Saudi Arabia?

As of November 2026, Google Cloud runs `me-central2` in Dammam (sold to Saudi-billed customers through CNTXT), Azure lists Saudi Arabia East in Dammam as a preview region, and AWS has announced Saudi regions that do not yet appear in its region list. Confirm current status with the provider before you design around it.

### Does web hosting include business email?

No. Web hosting and email hosting are separate purchases, and this is the most common budgeting omission. Business email is priced per user per month by providers such as Google Workspace and Microsoft 365. A free webmail address as a company's public contact signals to business buyers that the company is not established.

### How do I move hosting without losing my search rankings?

Lower DNS TTL at least a week ahead, copy every redirect rule, keep the old server running until its traffic reaches zero, and verify SSL on the new host before switching DNS. Immediately after the move, check that `robots.txt` does not block crawling, confirm Search Console verification still works, and watch index coverage for two weeks.
