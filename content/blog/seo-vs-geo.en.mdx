# SEO vs GEO: How AI Search Engines Are Reshaping Digital Visibility

**Generative engine optimisation (GEO) is the practice of making a page easy for AI answer engines — Google AI Overviews and AI Mode, ChatGPT search, Perplexity — to find, understand and quote, so that the page is cited inside the generated answer rather than only listed as a link.**

GEO does not replace SEO. Google's own [guide to generative AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide) states that "optimizing for generative AI search is optimizing for the search experience, and thus still SEO", and that no special files, markup or schema are needed. What changes is the unit of success: the sentence that gets quoted, not only the page that gets ranked.

![Illustration comparing classic search results with an AI-generated answer](/images/blog/seo-vs-geo/seo-vs-geo-hero.jpeg)

---

## The short version

| | SEO | GEO |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Rank a page in a list of results | Have a passage quoted or cited inside an AI answer |
| Unit that wins | The page | A self-contained sentence, figure or definition |
| Who reads it first | Search crawlers and the ranking system | The same crawlers, then a language model that summarises |
| Success metric | Clicks and impressions | Citations, mentions and the clicks that still follow them |
| Foundation | Crawlable, indexed, useful content | Exactly the same — plus passages specific enough to quote |

The rest of this article is the evidence for that table and the concrete changes it implies.

---

## Why it matters now: fewer clicks when an answer appears

The clearest measurement so far comes from [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/), which tracked the browsing of 900 U.S. adults in March 2025. When a Google results page showed an AI summary, users clicked a traditional result in **8% of visits**, against **15%** when no summary appeared. They clicked a link inside the summary itself in only **1%** of visits. About 18% of the searches in the study produced an AI summary.

Two conclusions follow. Ranking alone now delivers less traffic for the queries that trigger an answer. And when your page *is* the cited source, you are among the very few links that still get a click.

## What SEO is, and why GEO still depends on it

SEO is the work of making pages that search engines can crawl, index and rank for the queries your customers type. Classic search works in three stages:

![Diagram of the crawl, index and rank stages of a search engine](/images/blog/seo-vs-geo/search-engine-workflow.jpeg)

1. **Crawling:** the engine discovers and downloads pages.
2. **Indexing:** it stores and understands their content.
3. **Ranking:** it orders indexed pages by relevance and quality for each query.

AI answer engines sit on top of the same stages. Google [says](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) that to be eligible for AI Overviews and AI Mode a page must be indexed and eligible to show with a snippet, and that "there are no additional technical requirements." A page that is blocked, unindexed or invisible to the crawler cannot be quoted, however well it is written. That is why GEO is a layer on top of SEO rather than a replacement for it.

## What GEO adds: content specific enough to quote

![Illustration of an AI system reading several sources and composing one answer](/images/blog/seo-vs-geo/ai-search-concept.jpeg)

An answer engine retrieves several sources and writes one response. It quotes the passages that answer the question directly and verifiably. The best evidence on what makes a passage get picked is the [GEO study by Aggarwal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09735), published at KDD 2024, which tested nine rewriting methods against a benchmark of 10,000 queries:

| Change to the source text | Effect on visibility in generated answers |
|---|---|
| Adding quotations from relevant sources | about **+41%** |
| Adding statistics | about **+31%** |
| Citing sources | about **+27%** |
| Writing in a more authoritative tone | about **+10%** |
| Keyword stuffing | about **−8%** |

*Measured on the paper's position-adjusted word count metric, with a GPT-3.5 based engine; the authors note the effect varies by domain.*

The pattern is the opposite of old-school keyword optimisation: evidence makes a passage more quotable; repetition makes it less so. Google's [people-first content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) points the same way — of experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust, "trust is most important."

## Seven things a site can concretely do

### 1. Put a one-sentence answer under every question heading

Write the answer first, in a sentence that still makes sense when lifted out of the page: the name, the number, the rule. Put the detail and the caveats after it. This is the single change most directly tied to being quoted, because an answer engine needs a passage it can reuse without the surrounding paragraphs.

### 2. Back every figure with a linked primary source

Statistics and citations were two of the three strongest methods in the GEO study. Link the regulator's page, the vendor's documentation or the original study — not a blog that summarises it — and give the year. "Industry reports show" is neither quotable nor trustworthy.

### 3. Serve important content as server-rendered text

Google [processes JavaScript](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/javascript/javascript-seo-basics) in a separate rendering queue and still calls server-side or pre-rendering "a great idea because it makes your website faster for users and crawlers", noting that "not all bots can run JavaScript." Its AI features page also asks that important content be "available in textual form." In practice: the answer should be in the HTML the server sends, not assembled in the browser or locked inside an image. Platform choice decides this early — see [WordPress vs Next.js](/en/blog/wordpress-vs-nextjs).

### 4. Use descriptive headings with stable anchors

Give each section an H2 or H3 that states the question or topic in plain words, and give each heading an `id` so it has its own URL fragment. A clear outline lets both crawlers and readers jump straight to the passage that answers them, and lets you link to one answer rather than a whole page. Google also says there is no need to break content into tiny pieces and "there's no ideal page length" — structure for the reader, not for an imagined chunk size.

### 5. Let the right crawlers in

Check `robots.txt`, your CDN and any bot-protection layer. Google requires that crawling be allowed for AI Overviews eligibility. For ChatGPT search, OpenAI's [crawler documentation](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots) says sites should allow **OAI-SearchBot**; blocking it removes you from ChatGPT search answers. Its separate **GPTBot** crawler is for model training, so you can block training while still allowing search.

### 6. Keep structured data accurate — but know what it does today

Google's guide is explicit: "Structured data isn't required for generative AI search, and there's no special schema.org markup you need to add." It still recommends structured data for ordinary SEO, and asks that it match the visible text. Note one change in 2026: Google [stopped showing FAQ rich results](https://developers.google.com/search/updates) from 7 May 2026. FAQ markup no longer earns an expanded result in Google; a visible, well-written FAQ section with question headings still gives every engine discrete, quotable answers.

### 7. Treat llms.txt as optional

[llms.txt](https://llmstxt.org/) is a proposal, published by Jeremy Howard in September 2024, for a Markdown file at the site root that gives language-model tools a curated map of a site. It is not a standard, and Google's guide says plainly that Google Search "doesn't use" such files. It costs little to publish one for other tools and agents that read it; it will not move your Google visibility.

## Writing the same page two ways

Take two service pages on the same topic.

**Page one** is tuned for keywords: the target phrase in the title, the meta description and every second paragraph, with general claims such as "we deliver high-quality solutions." It may rank, but there is nothing in it an answer engine can quote — no number, no definition, no named source.

**Page two** opens with a one-sentence answer, gives a table with concrete figures, cites the regulator or vendor page behind each figure, and closes with a FAQ whose questions are headings. Every one of those elements matches a method that the GEO study found increases visibility, and none of them conflicts with Google's guidance.

Measure the difference on your own site rather than borrowing someone else's numbers: Google's guide points to the generative AI performance data in Search Console, and your analytics will show referral traffic from AI assistants' domains.

## Will GEO replace SEO?

No. Google describes optimising for its AI features as SEO, and every answer engine still has to crawl and index a page before it can quote it. GEO is the discipline of writing pages that are worth quoting once they are found; SEO is still what gets them found.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a separate GEO strategy for Google AI Overviews?

No. Google says there are no additional requirements or special optimisations for AI Overviews or AI Mode: the page must be indexed and eligible for a snippet. The useful work is the same as for search — crawlable text, specific answers and trustworthy sources.

### Does FAQ schema still help after Google removed FAQ rich results?

It no longer produces an FAQ rich result in Google, which stopped showing them from 7 May 2026, and Google says no special schema is needed for its AI features. A visible FAQ section with question headings and one-sentence answers still helps every engine find a quotable passage; keep any markup accurate to the visible text.

### Should I publish an llms.txt file?

Publish one if it is cheap for you, but do not expect it to change your Google visibility: Google's guide says Google Search does not use llms.txt. It is an informal proposal that some other AI tools and agents read.

### Which GEO changes have measured evidence behind them?

In the KDD 2024 GEO study, adding quotations raised visibility in generated answers by about 41%, adding statistics by about 31% and citing sources by about 27%; keyword stuffing reduced it by about 8%. Results varied by subject area, so test on your own pages.

### Can I block AI training but still appear in ChatGPT search?

Yes. OpenAI uses OAI-SearchBot for ChatGPT search and GPTBot for model training. Allow OAI-SearchBot and disallow GPTBot in `robots.txt`; OpenAI says such changes take about 24 hours to apply to search.

### How do I measure whether AI engines cite my pages?

Use the generative AI performance data in Google Search Console, watch analytics for referrals from AI assistants' domains, and periodically ask the questions your customers ask in ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google to see which sources are cited. No tool yet gives a complete picture across all engines.

## Where to start

Pick the ten pages that answer your customers' most common questions. For each, write a one-sentence answer under a question heading, add the figure and its primary source, and check that the text arrives in the server's HTML. That is a week of work for most sites, and it improves classic search at the same time.
