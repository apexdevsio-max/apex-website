# Freelancer or Development Agency? A Total Cost Comparison

**An independent freelancer charges $15–40 an hour; a regional or local development agency charges $50–100 an hour (our published rate card, as of November 2026).** For the same hours, the agency costs roughly 2.5 to 3.3 times as much. Choose a freelancer for small, well-scoped work, and an agency when the project is core to your business.

Comparing hourly rates alone is still misleading, because it ignores what happens after delivery. The rest of this guide prices that in.

The rates are the two hourly bands from our published rate card; the "30–40%" row is simple arithmetic on them ($15 against $50, $40 against $100). If you are weighing a team for a full app, our [mobile app development service](/en/services/mobile-apps) page shows what an agency engagement covers; for a website, see [web development](/en/services/web-development).

## The real risk: a single point of failure

This is the fundamental difference, not price.

**A freelancer is one person.** If they fall ill, get absorbed by a bigger project, change career direction, or simply stop replying — **your project stops**. And if the code is undocumented (which is common), the next developer needs weeks to understand it and may propose a rebuild.

**An agency is a team.** One person's absence doesn't halt the project, knowledge is distributed, and the contract obliges delivery.

**How do you reduce this risk with a freelancer?**
- Require code delivery to a repository **you own**, weekly rather than at the end. On GitHub, a repository created under your organisation stays yours; one created under the developer's account has to be [transferred](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/creating-and-managing-repositories/transferring-a-repository) to you, which moves its issues, pull requests and history with it.
- Ask for basic documentation as part of delivery, not as an extra.
- Register the domain and store accounts **in your company's name** from day one. Apple shows the account holder's name [as the seller on the App Store](https://developer.apple.com/programs/enroll/), and Google Play requires an [organisation account](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/13634885) for some categories such as financial and health apps.
- Pay in instalments tied to deliverables, holding back 20% for the final one.

These four conditions convert most freelancer risk into containable risk. See our [app development contract guide](/en/blog/app-development-contract) for the clauses.

## Total cost over three years

The gap narrows once you count what comes after delivery. Take a scope of **400 hours** and price it from the rate card, with maintenance at the rate card's 15–20% of the build cost per year:

| Line item | Freelancer ($15–40/hr) | Agency ($50–100/hr) |
|---|---|---|
| Initial build (400 hours) | $6,000 – $16,000 | $20,000 – $40,000 |
| Maintenance, 3 years at 15–20% a year | $2,700 – $9,600 | $9,000 – $24,000 |
| **Three-year total** | **$8,700 – $25,600** | **$29,000 – $64,000** |
| If the build must be redone by an agency | add $20,000 – $40,000 | — |

*Ranges from our published rate card, as of November 2026. The 400 hours is an illustration; your own scope sets the hours.*

**The result: the freelancer remains cheaper** — unless the work has to be rebuilt. One rebuild puts the freelancer path at $28,700–65,600, the same as or more than hiring the agency in the first place. So the real question isn't money but **how likely a rebuild is, and who carries that risk**: with a freelancer, you; with an agency, they do (in theory, and to the extent the contract binds them).

The table also assumes both deliver the same work in the same hours. A freelancer working alone may take longer on a multi-disciplinary project, which narrows the gap further.

## When to choose a freelancer

- **A brochure site or landing page.**
- **A prototype (MVP)** to test an idea before major investment.
- **A specific task** — a fix, a feature addition, a performance improvement.
- **A genuinely limited budget** where the project isn't critical to your business.
- **You have internal technical capacity** to review and manage the work.

## When to choose an agency

- **The app or site is core to your business** — downtime stops your revenue.
- **The project is multi-disciplinary** — design + development + testing + integration.
- **You need contractual commitment** on deadlines, warranty, and support.
- **An enterprise project** with compliance and documentation requirements.
- **You have no time to manage the project** yourself.

## The third model: a small regional team

Often overlooked and usually the best fit for mid-size projects: **a small firm (5–15 staff) or a remote regional team**.

- **Price:** it sits in the same $50–100 an hour agency band (as of November 2026) — you are not paying freelancer rates, but you are not funding a large agency's management layers either.
- **Provides team continuity:** design, development and testing in one place, so one person's absence doesn't stop the work.
- **More flexible** than large organisations in dealing and revising.
- **The drawback:** quality varies more widely — checking the portfolio and references matters even more here.

If the team works remotely, ask about time-zone overlap and how often you will see working software. Our [guide to hiring a development team in the Gulf](/en/blog/gulf-app-development-guide) covers what to check with a remote regional team.

## One question that reveals the truth with either

**"Who will actually write the code?"**

With a freelancer: do they execute it themselves or pass it to others? Some "independents" are intermediaries redistributing work.

With an agency: is the team employed or contracted? Outsourcing isn't a flaw, but **concealing it means you pay an extra margin without knowing**.

A straight answer to this question reveals more than any presentation.

## How to evaluate either one

The same standards apply to both — don't lower the bar for a freelancer because they cost less:

**1. Published, verifiable work.** Links that actually work, not images in a profile. Open the project and use it yourself.

**2. References you can speak to.** Ask one decisive question: was the project delivered on time, and how did they handle the delay if there was one?

**3. Clarity in scoping.** Anyone who gives you a price before understanding your requirements in detail is guessing — and guesses turn into disputes later.

**4. Disciplined communication during the proposal stage.** If they are slow to reply before the contract, they won't improve after it.

**5. Willingness to say "no".** Someone who agrees to every request without discussing whether it is worth doing is selling you hours, not solutions.

> **A shared warning sign:** refusing to provide a written contract. Freelancer or agency, an agreement made only in chat messages leaves everything open to interpretation when a dispute arises.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much more does an agency charge per hour than a freelancer?

On our rate card (as of November 2026), a freelancer charges $15–40 an hour and a regional or local agency $50–100, so the same hours cost roughly 2.5 to 3.3 times as much at an agency. Over three years, including maintenance at 15–20% of the build per year, the freelancer usually stays cheaper unless the work has to be rebuilt.

### How do I protect myself when working with a freelancer?

Four conditions: weekly code delivery to a repository you own, basic documentation within the delivery, domain and store accounts registered in your company's name from the start, and payments tied to deliverables with 20% held back. These convert most risks into containable ones.

### What if the freelancer disappears mid-project?

If you applied the conditions above, you own the code completed to date and another developer can finish it. If you didn't, you may lose everything and start over. That probability is what you should price into your decision — a single rebuild costs as much as the agency would have.

### Does an agency guarantee higher quality?

Not automatically. A bad agency is worse than an excellent freelancer. What an agency guarantees is **continuity and contractual accountability**, not quality itself. Check the portfolio and references in both cases by the same standards.

### Can I start with a freelancer and move to an agency later?

Yes, and it's a common, sensible path: a prototype with a freelancer to test the idea, then an agency to build the production version. The condition is owning the code, the store accounts and the documentation from the first stage — otherwise moving means rebuilding.

### Is it a mistake to hire the cheapest freelancer for a revenue-critical app?

Usually, yes. If the site or app going down stops your revenue, the saving on the build doesn't compensate for a week of downtime with nobody to fix it. For revenue-critical work, continuity is what you are buying.

## The decision in four lines

**A freelancer** for small, well-scoped projects and prototypes — $15–40 an hour, provided you apply the four conditions that protect you.

**An agency** when the project is core to your business or multi-disciplinary — $50–100 an hour, paying for continuity and accountability, not for code alone.

**A small regional team** inside that agency band is usually the best fit for mid-size projects.

**And ask both the same question:** who will actually write the code?
