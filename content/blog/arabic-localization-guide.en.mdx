# Arabic Localisation for Apps and Websites: The Complete Guide

Arabic localisation is six layers of work — translation, right-to-left direction, typography, numerals and formats, dates, and content adaptation — and translation is only one of them. Teams that budget for translation alone ship products Arabic users describe as "translated" rather than "Arabic". The fix is to plan the other five from the first sprint: logical CSS or directional widgets, an Arabic typeface with zero letter-spacing, one digit system, Hijri display where it matters, and copy written for Arabic readers.

This guide covers the full scope, what drives the budget, and the specific failures that make an Arabic interface feel wrong. Each technical area has a dedicated deep-dive linked from here. If you are planning a build rather than an audit, Arabic is part of every product we scope in our [mobile app](/en/services/mobile-apps) and [web development](/en/services/web-development) services.

## Six layers, not one

Localisation failures are almost always scope failures — a team plans for layer one and discovers the other five during QA.

| Layer | What it involves | Commonly missed? |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Translation** | Text converted to Arabic | Planned for |
| **2. Direction (RTL)** | Layout, navigation, icons mirrored | Underestimated |
| **3. Typography** | Arabic fonts, sizing, line height | Usually missed |
| **4. Numerals and formats** | Digits, currency, phone numbers | Often missed |
| **5. Dates** | Hijri alongside Gregorian | Often missed |
| **6. Content adaptation** | Tone, examples, imagery, cultural fit | Rarely considered |

A product that handles only layers 1 and 2 is what users mean by "translated". Layers 3 to 6 are what make it feel built for them.

## Layer 2: direction is not a CSS property

The instinct is to set `direction: rtl` and consider it done. The W3C's [guidance on right-to-left HTML](https://www.w3.org/International/questions/qa-html-dir) is explicit that base direction belongs in markup — `dir="rtl"` on the `html` element, alongside `lang="ar"` — not in CSS. Then decide what actually mirrors:

**Mirrors:** overall layout, text alignment, navigation order, progress bars, sliders, carousels, back/forward arrows, dropdown alignment, table column order, and drawer position.

**Does not mirror:** clocks, media playback controls (play points right in every language), charts with a time axis, phone numbers, email addresses, code, and most logos.

That second list is the one that causes bugs. A blanket mirror flips the play button and makes the video player nonsensical.

**The engineering habit that prevents most of this:** use logical properties rather than physical ones — `margin-inline-start` instead of `margin-left`, `padding-inline-end` instead of `padding-right`. Written this way the layout mirrors correctly by default, instead of through a parallel RTL stylesheet that drifts out of sync. [RTL in CSS](/en/blog/rtl-css-guide) covers the full mapping.

For apps, [Arabic and RTL support in Flutter](/en/blog/flutter-rtl-arabic-guide) covers directional widgets and icon mirroring, and [Arabic and RTL in React Native](/en/blog/react-native-rtl-arabic) covers `I18nManager`. Our own shipped example is [Tafawwoq](/en/portfolio/tafawwoq-educational-app), an Arabic educational app built in Flutter with Clean Architecture and BLoC and a full Arabic RTL interface.

## Layer 3: Arabic typography is not Latin typography resized

Arabic script is cursive — letters join and change shape by position. That has consequences most design systems do not account for:

**Letter-spacing must be zero.** MDN's [`letter-spacing` reference](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/letter-spacing) warns that Arabic-script text expects connected letters to stay connected and that letter spacing "may lead to the text looking broken". This is one of the most common CSS mistakes on bilingual sites, usually inherited from a heading style designed for Latin type.

**Line height must increase.** Arabic needs more vertical room than Latin; a `line-height` that looks comfortable in English feels cramped in Arabic. Start around 1.7 to 1.8 for body text and tune by eye. [Arabic web fonts](/en/blog/arabic-web-fonts) explains why, with the W3C's layout requirements as the reference.

**Size needs checking, not copying.** At the same point size Arabic often reads smaller than Latin. Compare the two languages side by side at real sizes rather than reusing the English scale unchanged.

**Text length changes unpredictably.** Arabic strings can be shorter or longer than their English equivalents and render taller. Fixed-height buttons and cards sized around English text break; use minimum heights and test with real Arabic copy.

**Font files are heavier.** Arabic typefaces carry far more glyphs than Latin ones. [Arabic web fonts](/en/blog/arabic-web-fonts) covers subsetting, `woff2` and `font-display` so this does not wreck your load time.

## Layer 4: numerals, the detail that quietly signals "foreign"

Arabic interfaces use two digit sets: Western digits (`1234`) and Eastern Arabic digits (`١٢٣٤`). The W3C's [Arabic layout requirements](https://www.w3.org/TR/alreq/) record Eastern Arabic (Arabic-Indic) digits as the set used in countries such as Egypt, Saudi Arabia and Iraq, and European digits in the Maghreb; in Gulf interfaces you will see both. Which to use is a **product decision**.

**Choose deliberately, then apply consistently.** Mixing them in one interface is the actual error. A price in `١٢٣` beside an order number in `123` reads as an unfinished product.

**Never mirror these,** whatever you choose: phone numbers, IBANs, card numbers, version numbers and anything else where digit order carries meaning. Keep them in Western digits and isolate them as left-to-right text.

**Also part of this layer:** currency placement and decimals (the Kuwaiti dinar, Bahraini dinar and Omani rial use three), decimal separators and percent sign position. These are locale settings handled by `Intl`, not translations. [Arabic numerals, currency and formats](/en/blog/arabic-numerals-formats) has the code.

## Layer 5: dates

Hijri dates matter in the Gulf, particularly for government-facing, religious and HR products.

**The rule that prevents most bugs: store Gregorian, display Hijri.** There is no single Hijri calendar: JavaScript's `Intl` exposes `islamic-umalqura`, `islamic-civil` and `islamic-tbla` as separate calendars, and MDN's [list of supported values](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/JavaScript/Reference/Global_Objects/Intl/supportedValuesOf) notes that the generic `islamic` value matches no specific real-world variant. Storing Hijri dates means storing that ambiguity.

[Hijri dates in applications](/en/blog/hijri-dates-guide) covers conversion in the browser and in Flutter, and why choosing the Umm al-Qura calendar explicitly matters for Saudi users.

## Layer 6: what translation alone never fixes

This is the layer that separates products that feel Arabic from products that feel translated, and it is almost never in scope.

**Search behaviour differs, not just vocabulary.** Arabic speakers phrase queries differently and often mix Arabic and English in one query. Literally translating your English keywords produces content nobody searches for — which is why [we treat Arabic content as an original rather than a translation](/en/blog/seo-vs-geo). Google also [recommends separate URLs per language version with hreflang annotations](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/managing-multi-regional-sites), rather than switching language by cookie or browser settings.

**Formality register is a real decision.** Modern Standard Arabic reads as formal and works everywhere. Dialect feels warmer but fragments your audience — Gulf, Levantine and Egyptian dialects differ substantially. Most products should default to MSA with light regional vocabulary; consumer products aimed at a single country can go further.

**Examples and imagery need adapting.** Names, cities, currencies and photographs of people all carry signals. An interface using "John" and dollar amounts in a Saudi product is technically translated and practically foreign.

**Diacritics (tashkeel) are usually wrong to include.** They clutter body text for fluent readers. Reserve them for children's content, religious text and genuinely ambiguous words.

## A phased plan

**Phase 1 — Foundation (before writing UI code)**
Set up the i18n framework, externalise every string, use logical CSS properties or directional widgets throughout, and test the layout in RTL early, even with placeholder text. Catching a broken layout in week two is trivial; catching it in month four is not.

**Phase 2 — Core localisation**
Professional translation of UI strings, Arabic font selection and subsetting, numeral and date formatting, and RTL verification screen by screen.

**Phase 3 — Content adaptation**
Rewrite marketing copy rather than translating it, adapt examples and imagery, and do Arabic keyword research independently of the English set.

**Phase 4 — QA by a native speaker**
This is not optional and it is not proofreading. A fluent Arabic reader using the product on a real device finds what no automated check catches — awkward line breaks, wrong register, icons mirrored that should not be, and text that is technically correct but reads as machine-produced.

## The failures worth naming

1. **`letter-spacing` applied to Arabic** — breaks letter joins and renders text visually broken.
2. **A separate RTL stylesheet** — drifts out of sync with the LTR one. Use logical properties instead.
3. **Mirroring things that should not mirror** — play buttons, clocks, phone numbers.
4. **Mixed numeral systems** in one interface.
5. **Hard-coded strings** discovered in QA, scattered across the codebase.
6. **Fixed-height components** that clip when Arabic text renders taller.
7. **Machine translation shipped without native review** — the fastest way to make a product feel unserious.
8. **Storing Hijri dates** instead of converting at display time.
9. **Ignoring mixed content** — Arabic sentences containing English product names or codes need bidirectional isolation, or punctuation lands in the wrong place.
10. **Treating Arabic SEO as translated English keywords** — the queries are genuinely different.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does it cost to build an app or website in Arabic?

Our rate card prices Arabic inside the normal build tiers rather than as an add-on. As of November 2026, a mid-size company website is $8,000–$25,000 (SAR 30,000–94,000), a simple app $5,000–$15,000 (SAR 19,000–56,500) and a mid-complexity app $15,000–$45,000 (SAR 56,500–169,000). Retrofitting Arabic into a shipped English-only product costs more, because layout assumptions have to be undone first.

### Is machine translation good enough for an Arabic app?

As a first draft for high-volume content, it can help. Shipping it without native review produces text fluent readers quickly identify as machine-produced, and it cannot make register decisions or adapt examples, which is where most of the perceived quality lives.

### What is the first thing to set for an RTL website?

`dir="rtl"` and `lang="ar"` on the `html` element. The W3C recommends setting base direction in markup rather than with CSS `direction`, and `lang` drives font selection and screen-reader pronunciation. Then write layout with logical properties so one stylesheet works in both directions.

### Should my Arabic interface use Eastern Arabic digits (١٢٣) or Western digits (123)?

Either is correct; the error is mixing them. Western digits are the lower-risk default for commercial products serving several Gulf countries, because identifiers such as phone numbers and IBANs are already written in them. Whatever you choose, keep phone numbers, IBANs and card numbers in Western digits and never mirror them.

### Do I need Hijri dates?

For government-facing, HR and religious products, often as the primary display; for most consumer products, as a secondary display beside Gregorian. Always store Gregorian and convert for display, and name the calendar explicitly (`islamic-umalqura` for Saudi users) because the Hijri calendar variants can differ by a day.

### Should I write in Modern Standard Arabic or dialect?

Modern Standard Arabic for most products: it reads as professional and works across every Arabic-speaking market. Dialect feels warmer but fragments your audience. A reasonable default is MSA with light regional vocabulary.

### Can I launch in English and add Arabic later?

Yes, if you build for it now: externalise every string into an i18n layer and use logical CSS properties or directional widgets from the first component. That costs a few days at the start. Skipping it means the later Arabic release starts with an audit of every screen.
