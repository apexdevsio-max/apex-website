# AI in Online Stores: What Increases Sales and What Doesn't

**For an Arabic online store, fix product search first, add recommendations second, use AI to draft product descriptions third, and only then build a support bot; visual search and dynamic pricing come last. The first two increase revenue; the next two reduce cost. As of November 2026, each model-based feature is AI/ML integration work at $10,000 – $40,000+ on our rate card — but the biggest search fix, Arabic normalisation, needs no model at all.**

Many stores buy AI features in the opposite order — starting with a chatbot because it is visible, while the search box keeps returning nothing for products that are in stock.

This guide ranks the features, with costs from our rate card. If you are building or rebuilding the store itself, our [web development services](/en/services/web-development) cover that side and our [AI solutions](/en/services/ai-solutions) the model-based features; for evaluating AI projects in general, see [AI for business](/en/blog/ai-for-business-guide).

## The build order

| Feature | What it changes | Needs a model call? | Build order |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Improved product search** | Revenue — shoppers find what you stock | Normalisation and typo tolerance: no. Semantic search: yes | First |
| **Product recommendations** | Revenue — order value | Rules: no. Learned recommendations: yes | Second |
| **Product description drafting** | Cost — copywriting time | Yes | Third |
| **Customer service bot** | Cost — support time | Yes | Fourth |
| **Visual / image search** | Situational; mainly fashion and home décor | Yes | Last |
| **Dynamic pricing** | Margin, with reputational risk | Usually | Rarely |

The ordering reflects a simple asymmetry: **search and recommendations increase revenue; description generation and support bots reduce cost.** Revenue features are worth more, and they are the ones most often left until later.

## Search is where Arabic stores lose sales

A shopper who searches has told you exactly what they want. If the search returns nothing for a product you stock, there is nothing for them to buy — and Arabic search fails this way far more easily than English.

**Why Arabic search fails:**

Arabic has multiple written forms of the same word. A shopper searching `احمد` will not match a product stored as `أحمد` unless you normalise. Taa marbuta versus haa, alef maksura versus yaa, optional diacritics, and tatweel all produce misses on products that are definitely in your catalogue.

**Then there is the mixed-script problem.** Shoppers type `ايفون` and `iPhone` interchangeably, and `لابتوب` and `laptop`. If your catalogue stores one form, the other returns nothing.

**What to build, in order:**

1. **Arabic normalisation** on both the index and the query. Apache Lucene's [Arabic normaliser](https://lucene.apache.org/core/9_0_0/analysis/common/org/apache/lucene/analysis/ar/ArabicNormalizer.html) does exactly five things — hamza-on-alef to bare alef, taa marbuta to haa, alef maksura to yaa, and removal of diacritics and tatweel — and Elasticsearch's built-in [Arabic analyzer](https://www.elastic.co/docs/reference/text-analysis/analysis-lang-analyzer) chains it with Arabic stop words and stemming. It is configuration rather than AI, and it is the highest-return fix on this page. [Arabic search](/en/blog/arabic-search-guide) covers the details.
2. **Transliteration aliases** for brand and product names so both scripts match.
3. **Typo tolerance** — Arabic keyboard errors differ from English ones, so tune fuzzy matching on your real queries.
4. **Semantic search** for intent-based queries like "gift for a child" that no keyword matches. Combine it with keyword search rather than replacing it: Anthropic's retrieval tests found embeddings plus BM25 keyword search beat embeddings alone, because [keyword matching catches exact terms](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval) such as product codes.

> **The diagnostic that pays for itself:** log every search returning zero results, then read that list weekly. It tells you exactly which normalisation cases you are missing and which products shoppers want that you do not stock. It is a few hours of work.

## Recommendations, honestly

Recommendations can lift average order value — but placement and data matter more than the algorithm, and only an A/B test tells you whether yours do.

**Where to try them first:**
- **"Frequently bought together"** on the product page
- **"Similar products"** when an item is out of stock, which offers an alternative instead of a dead end
- **Post-purchase suggestions** in confirmation emails

**Where they tend to underperform:**
- Generic "you might also like" carousels on the homepage
- Recommendations on a first visit with no behavioural data

**The cold-start problem is real.** A learned recommendation engine needs behavioural data. A new store, or a catalogue with few orders per product, has nothing to learn from. **Start with rules** — same category, complementary items, manually curated pairs — which need no model calls, and move to learned recommendations once you have the order volume to support them.

**Measure incremental lift, not clicks.** Recommendations that get clicked but replace purchases the shopper would have made anyway add nothing. The honest test is an A/B comparison against no recommendations.

## Product descriptions

The clearest cost-saving case in e-commerce AI, and the easiest to get wrong.

**Why it works:** writing five hundred product descriptions is expensive and slow. Generation produces a usable first draft in seconds.

**Why it fails:** published without review. Language models can state a material, a dimension, or a compatibility claim that is simply wrong — Anthropic's own guidance on [reducing hallucinations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations) notes the techniques reduce errors but "don't eliminate them entirely." In a store, a wrong specification is a returns problem and potentially a consumer protection issue.

**How to do it properly:**
- Give the model **real specifications** as input and instruct it to use only those, rather than asking it to describe from a product name
- Treat output as a **draft requiring approval**, not as published copy
- **Write Arabic descriptions as originals**, not translations of English ones — shoppers phrase searches differently in each language, and a translated description matches queries nobody types
- Watch for repetitive phrasing across the catalogue, which reads as machine-generated at scale

## What to build last, or not at all

**Visual search** — photograph an item, find similar products. Impressive, and mainly relevant where shoppers browse by look, such as fashion and home décor. It is expensive to build and maintain; build it when text search is already excellent, not before.

**Dynamic pricing** — algorithmic price adjustment by demand. Technically feasible and commercially risky: shoppers notice, screenshot, and share. Where price fairness matters to your reputation, the downside can exceed the margin gain.

**A chatbot before your FAQ is good.** Many store support questions are "where is my order?" and "what is your return policy?" Order tracking and a clear returns page answer both more reliably and far more cheaply than a bot. [AI chatbot costs](/en/blog/ai-chatbot-cost) covers when a bot does earn its cost — after these basics, not instead of them.

Ranges from our published rate card, as of November 2026; the integration tier is open-ended at the top. Normalised keyword search and rule-based recommendations are ordinary development work inside the store build, not AI integration.

**Estimate the return before building.** For search, the calculation is unusually clean:

```
monthly visitors using search × current conversion rate
vs. the same with an improved conversion rate
× average order value
```

An illustration with round numbers: a store with 10,000 monthly search users, a 2% search conversion rate, and a $60 average order value earns about $12,000 a month from search. **Improving that by a fifth adds $2,400 a month** — enough to pay back a $10,000 integration, the bottom of the tier, in a little over four months. Use your own analytics, not these figures.

**Do the same arithmetic for recommendations** using order-value uplift from an A/B test, and for descriptions using hours of copywriting saved.

**Running costs** apply to anything calling a model — semantic search, description generation, and chatbots all charge per use. Rules-based recommendations and normalised keyword search do not, which is another argument for doing those first. [What AI costs to run](/en/blog/llm-running-costs) covers estimating this.

## Compliance

**Recommendation engines profile shoppers**, which is processing of personal data under Gulf data protection law. Confirm your legal basis with counsel before launch, especially if the profiles also drive marketing — [PDPL compliance](/en/blog/pdpl-compliance-guide) sets out the Saudi rules.

**Sending product data to an external model is low-risk** — a product description is not personal data. **Sending customer data is not.** Purchase histories, browsing behaviour, and support conversations all carry obligations.

**Check the training question** before wiring up any provider. On the API, providers such as [Anthropic](https://privacy.claude.com/en/articles/7996868-is-my-data-used-for-model-training) state that inputs are not used for training by default; consumer tools and some third-party plugins have different terms. See [Gulf regulatory compliance](/en/blog/gulf-compliance-guide).

## Frequently asked questions

### Which AI feature should an online store build first?

Search — and for an Arabic store, the first step is Arabic normalisation of the catalogue and the queries, which needs no model at all. Recommendations come second, starting with simple rules. Description drafting and a support bot save cost rather than add revenue, so they come after; visual search and dynamic pricing come last.

### Why does my Arabic store search miss products that exist?

Arabic has several written forms of the same word — hamza on alef, taa marbuta versus haa, alef maksura versus yaa, optional diacritics, tatweel — so a shopper's query and your product title may not compare as equal even though they read identically. Normalise both the index and the query (Elasticsearch's Arabic analyzer does this out of the box), and add transliteration aliases so `iPhone` and `ايفون` both match.

### Do AI product recommendations actually increase sales?

They can, but only an A/B test against no recommendations tells you whether yours do. Try "frequently bought together" on product pages and "similar products" for out-of-stock items first; generic homepage carousels tend to underperform. Count incremental orders, not clicks — a clicked recommendation that replaces a purchase already intended adds nothing.

### Can I use AI to write product descriptions?

Yes, as a first draft with human approval before publishing. Give the model the real specifications and tell it to use only those; language models can still state a wrong material or dimension, and in a store that becomes a returns problem. Write Arabic descriptions as originals rather than translations of the English.

### Is visual search worth building for my store?

Usually not first. It matters mainly where shoppers browse by look, such as fashion and home décor, and it costs considerably more than text search to build and maintain. Make text search excellent before considering it.

### How do I calculate whether AI search pays for itself?

Multiply monthly search users by search conversion rate by average order value to get current revenue from search, then model an improved conversion rate. With round numbers — 10,000 search users, 2% conversion, $60 orders — search earns about $12,000 a month, and a one-fifth improvement adds $2,400 a month, paying back a $10,000 integration in a little over four months.

### Are there compliance issues with recommendation engines?

Yes. Recommendations profile shoppers, which is processing of personal data under Gulf data protection law, so confirm the legal basis with counsel before launch — particularly if the profiles feed marketing. Sending product data to an external model is low-risk; sending purchase histories or support conversations is not.
