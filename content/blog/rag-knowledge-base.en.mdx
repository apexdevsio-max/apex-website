# RAG: Making AI Answer From Your Own Documents

**RAG — retrieval-augmented generation — retrieves the relevant passages from your own documents and gives them to the model as context, so it answers from your material rather than from memory. Its quality is decided mainly by retrieval, not by the model: Anthropic cut failed retrievals by 49% purely by changing how chunks were prepared and searched. As of November 2026, building one falls in the AI/ML integration tier of our rate card, $10,000 – $40,000+.**

Without it, a language model knows nothing about your refund policy, your product catalogue, or the contract you signed last March. Ask anyway and you get a fluent, confident answer that is invented — worse than no answer at all.

This guide covers how it works, what breaks, and how Arabic changes the calculation. For whether an AI project is worth building at all, start with [AI for business](/en/blog/ai-for-business-guide); for help scoping one, see our [AI solutions](/en/services/ai-solutions).

## How it actually works

The technique was named in a [2020 paper by Lewis et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401), which paired a language model with a searchable vector index of documents. In a business system it is four steps, and only one of them involves the model.

**1. Indexing (once, then on updates).** Your documents are split into passages and converted into vectors — numerical representations that place similar meanings near each other. These are stored in a vector database.

**2. Retrieval (every question).** The user's question is converted the same way, and the system finds the passages closest to it in meaning.

**3. Augmentation.** Those passages are inserted into the prompt: *"Answer using only the following context…"*

**4. Generation.** The model answers from what it was given.

**The critical consequence:** if step 2 retrieves the wrong passages, the model answers confidently from the wrong material. Swapping in a better model does not fix it. **When a RAG system disappoints, check retrieval first.**

---

## Chunking decides more than model choice

How you split documents is the highest-leverage decision in the whole system, and it is often made carelessly.

**Chunks too large:** retrieval returns a page when the answer is one sentence. The model gets diluted context, costs rise, and accuracy falls.

**Chunks too small:** a passage loses the context that makes it meaningful. "The fee is 5%" is useless without knowing which fee.

**What works in practice:**

- **Split on structure, not character count.** Headings, sections, and clauses are natural boundaries. Splitting every 500 characters cuts sentences in half.
- **Overlap adjacent chunks** modestly (10–20% is a reasonable starting point) so an answer spanning a boundary is not lost.
- **Keep the context with the chunk.** A passage carrying "Section 4: Refund Policy" retrieves far better than the same text bare. Anthropic's [contextual retrieval](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval) experiments added a short explanation of where each chunk sits in its document and cut failed retrievals by 35% with embeddings alone.
- **Store metadata** — source document, section, date. It powers filtering and, more importantly, citations.

> **The test that tells you the truth:** take twenty real user questions and check whether the correct passage appears in the top three retrieved results. If it does not, no model will save you — fix chunking first.

## Arabic changes the retrieval problem

Two issues make Arabic RAG harder, and neither shows up in an English pilot.

**Embedding quality varies by language and provider.** The models that convert text to vectors do not handle Arabic equally well, and published benchmarks rarely match your domain. **Test embedding models on your own Arabic content** — it is the single most valuable evaluation you can run.

**The same word has several written forms.** Hamza on alef, taa marbuta versus haa, alef maksura versus yaa, optional diacritics and tatweel mean a question and the document may not match even when they say the same thing. Apache Lucene's [Arabic normaliser](https://lucene.apache.org/core/9_0_0/analysis/common/org/apache/lucene/analysis/ar/ArabicNormalizer.html) applies exactly these five rules; apply the same to both sides before indexing. [Arabic search](/en/blog/arabic-search-guide) covers the transformations in detail.

**Mixed-script content** is normal in real documents: Arabic prose with English product names. Index both scripts in the same field so a mixed question matches.

**Run hybrid retrieval** — combine vector similarity with traditional keyword (BM25) search. Anthropic found embeddings plus BM25 retrieve better than embeddings alone, because keyword matching catches exact terms such as codes and names that embeddings miss; together with contextual chunks the reduction in failed retrievals reached 49%. On Arabic content, where normalised keywords and product codes carry a lot of the meaning, this is the default to start from.

## Citations are not optional

The single most valuable feature you can add, and the cheapest.

**Show which document and section each answer came from.** This does three things:

1. **Users can verify.** A confident wrong answer is dangerous; a confident wrong answer with a link the user can check is a correctable error.
2. **You can debug.** When an answer is wrong, the citation tells you instantly whether retrieval failed or the model misread correct material.
3. **Trust is calibrated.** Users treat a cited answer as a research aid rather than an oracle, which is the correct mental model.

**Also handle "I don't know" properly.** Anthropic's [guidance on reducing hallucinations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations) starts with exactly this: explicitly allow the model to say it does not know, restrict it to the provided documents, and have it back each claim with a quote. Then test that it actually does. A system that invents an answer rather than admitting a gap is worse than a search box.

## When something simpler wins

**Use good search instead when** users are looking for a document rather than an answer. If the outcome is "show me the leave policy," a search box that returns the document is faster, cheaper, and cannot hallucinate. [Arabic search](/en/blog/arabic-search-guide) covers doing that well.

**Use a curated FAQ when** the questions are few and stable. Twenty common questions with written answers beat a RAG system on accuracy, cost, and maintenance — and take days rather than months.

**Put the content in the prompt when** your knowledge base is genuinely small. Anthropic's guidance is that a knowledge base under about 200,000 tokens can go straight into the prompt with no retrieval layer at all — simpler, and at that size usually more accurate.

**RAG earns its complexity when** the corpus is too large to fit in context, changes regularly, and users ask open-ended questions that a fixed FAQ cannot anticipate.

## How to evaluate it

Without a fixed test set, teams end up arguing about quality from anecdotes.

**Build a test set of 30–50 real questions** with known correct answers, drawn from actual user questions rather than invented ones.

**Measure retrieval and generation separately.** Retrieval: is the correct passage in the top results? Generation: given correct context, is the answer right? Separating these tells you which half to fix.

**Track refusals.** How often does it say "I don't know" when the answer was available? Too often and retrieval is weak; never, and it is probably inventing.

**Re-run after every change.** Chunking tweaks, model swaps, and prompt edits all shift quality in ways that are invisible without a fixed test set.

## Frequently asked questions

### How does RAG stop a model inventing company policy?

It finds the relevant passages in your own documents, inserts them into the prompt, and instructs the model to answer only from that material. Add an explicit instruction to say "I don't know" when the passages do not contain the answer, plus a citation to the source section, and the model has both the facts and a sanctioned way out. These steps reduce invented answers; they do not eliminate them, so test refusals.

### Why does my RAG system give wrong answers?

Check retrieval before the model. If the wrong passages are fetched, the model answers confidently from the wrong material, and a better model will not fix it. Test whether the correct passage appears in the top three results for twenty real questions — if it does not, fix chunking and retrieval first.

### How should I split documents for RAG?

On structure rather than character count — headings, sections, and clauses are natural boundaries. Overlap adjacent chunks modestly (10 to 20 percent is a reasonable start), attach the section heading or a short description of where the chunk sits in its document, and store metadata for filtering and citations.

### Does RAG work well in Arabic?

It works, but it needs two things English does not. First, normalise both documents and questions — hamza on alef, taa marbuta, alef maksura, diacritics, and tatweel — so equivalent spellings match. Second, run hybrid retrieval that combines vector search with keyword search, and choose the embedding model by testing it on your own Arabic content rather than trusting a general benchmark.

### How much does a RAG system cost to build?

As of November 2026, a RAG knowledge base added to an existing product sits in the AI/ML integration tier of our rate card: $10,000 – $40,000+ (SAR 37,500 – 150,000+). A standalone AI-powered app built around it is $40,000 – $150,000+. Running cost is per query and scales with chunk size, and re-indexing as documents change is the item most often left out of the budget.

### Is my knowledge base too small for RAG?

Possibly. If the whole knowledge base fits in under about 200,000 tokens, Anthropic's guidance is to put it directly in the prompt and skip retrieval. If users want a document rather than an answer, search is faster and cannot hallucinate; if the questions are few and stable, a curated FAQ beats RAG on accuracy and maintenance.
