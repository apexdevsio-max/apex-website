# Writing a Privacy Policy That Actually Works

**A privacy policy for a Gulf app has to satisfy three readers: the regulator, Apple and Google, and every one of them asks for the same core content.** That means who you are, what data you collect and why, your lawful basis, who receives the data and where it goes, how long you keep it and how you destroy it, and how users exercise their rights. In Saudi Arabia, Article 12 of the PDPL makes that policy mandatory at the point of collection; both app stores reject apps without one.

> Written from an engineering perspective, not legal advice. Current as of November 2026.

The rest of this guide is about writing that content from what your product really does, rather than from a template. For which law applies to you, see the [Gulf regulatory compliance guide](/en/blog/gulf-compliance-guide). ## What the law and the stores require

**Saudi PDPL.** [Article 12](https://dgp.sdaia.gov.sa/wps/portal/pdp/knowledgecenter/details/PDPL) requires the controller to adopt a privacy policy and make it available to data subjects when their data is collected. It must cover the purpose of collection, the data collected, how it is collected and stored, how it is processed and destroyed, and the data subject's rights and how to exercise them. Article 13 adds what you must tell people when you collect directly from them: the legal basis, the purpose and whether each item is mandatory or optional, your identity, who receives the data and whether it goes outside the Kingdom, the effect of not providing it, and their rights.

**SDAIA's guideline.** SDAIA's [guideline for preparing a privacy policy](https://dgp.sdaia.gov.sa/wps/portal/pdp/knowledgecenter/details/ElaborationandDevelopingPrivacyPolicyGuideline) lists the expected elements in more detail:

- The entity's official name and activities
- Contact details, an update log, and the data protection officer's details where one must be appointed
- Categories of personal data, and whether each is mandatory or optional
- Collection methods (direct and indirect), purposes and legal basis
- How the data is processed, and any disclosure to other parties
- Where data is stored, retention periods, destruction methods and security measures
- Data subject rights and how to exercise them
- How to complain or object, including to the competent authority

**Apple.** [Guideline 5.1.1(i)](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/) requires a privacy policy link in App Store Connect and an easily accessible link inside the app. The policy must identify what data the app collects, how, and every use of it; confirm that third parties receiving data give the same protection; and explain retention and deletion and how users revoke consent or request deletion.

**Google Play.** The [User data policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10144311) requires a privacy policy link in Play Console and a link or text inside the app, **even if the app accesses no personal data**. The policy must sit at a public, active URL that is not a PDF, name the developer or the app, give a privacy contact, describe the data accessed, collected, used and shared, and explain secure handling and retention and deletion.

## Why templates create liability

A privacy policy is a **statement of your actual practices**, not a statement of good intentions. A template describes someone else's product: cookies you do not set, vendors you do not use, retention periods you do not honour. Each of those is a written statement that does not match reality.

**Three concrete consequences:**

1. **It documents the gap.** If your policy promises deletion within 30 days and your system cannot delete, you have written down the evidence.
2. **The store declarations must agree with it.** Google says the Data safety section "must be consistent with the disclosures made in the app's privacy policy", and Apple makes you responsible for keeping your privacy details "accurate and up to date". A template that disagrees with your declarations puts you in breach of both.
3. **Enterprise buyers read it.** A policy naming a US-only provider while you pitch in-country hosting undermines the pitch.

**The honest test:** read your current policy and check whether each sentence is true of your product today.

## Derive it from a data map, not a template

Build the map first; the policy is a rendering of it.

**Step 1: list every data point you collect.** Work from the codebase and vendor list, not memory: registration fields, analytics identifiers, device information, location, uploads, support conversations, payment data.

**Step 2: record four things for each.**

| Column | Example |
|---|---|
| What | Email address |
| Why | Account authentication and service notifications |
| Lawful basis | Performing the agreement with the user |
| Retention | Life of the account plus 30 days |

**Step 3: list every third party that receives data.** Analytics, crash reporting, push notifications, email delivery, payments, hosting, AI APIs. Apple and Google both count SDKs you embed: Apple asks you to declare "the practices of third-party partners whose code you integrate", and Google's Data safety section must cover data collected by any SDK in your app.

**Step 4: note where each stores data.** That is what lets you write an accurate transfer statement, and Saudi Article 13 asks you to say whether data goes outside the Kingdom. [Data residency in the Gulf](/en/blog/data-residency-gulf) explains why location matters.

The map also answers enterprise security questionnaires and turns the next policy update into a short edit.

## Clauses people get wrong

**Purpose.** "To improve our services" is not a purpose. "To diagnose crashes and prioritise bug fixes" is.

**Lawful basis.** Do not default to consent for everything. The Saudi PDPL lets you process without consent in defined cases, including a legitimate interest that does not involve sensitive data, so state the basis you actually rely on. The [UAE data protection guide](/en/blog/uae-data-protection-guide) covers the bases there.

**Retention.** Give durations you can honour. "As long as necessary" only works if you then say what decides necessity.

**Rights.** Name the rights and the mechanism. Saudi controllers must answer requests within 30 days, extendable by up to 30 more with notice, under Article 3 of the [Implementing Regulations](https://dgp.sdaia.gov.sa/wps/portal/pdp/knowledgecenter/details/PDPL2). Apple and Google both require in-app account deletion if you offer account creation.

**Children.** Say whether the service is directed at minors and what you do about it. See [children's data protection](/en/blog/childrens-data-protection).

**Changes.** Say how you notify users. SDAIA's guideline expects an update log.

## Language: clear first, then Arabic

SDAIA's guideline does not require Arabic or English by name. It requires wording that is "clear, not misleading, and easy to read and understand", suited to the comprehension of all groups of data subjects, and recommends the language suited to the target audience. For an app aimed at Saudi or wider Gulf users, that points to Arabic, usually alongside English.

**Do not machine-translate it.** A legal page that reads as machine output undermines the trust it exists to build. Have a fluent reviewer check it.

**Use the regulator's terms.** The Saudi texts use established Arabic terms: جهة التحكم for controller, جهة المعالجة for processor, البيانات الحساسة for sensitive data. Using different words in your policy creates ambiguity.

**Say which version governs.** If you publish two languages, add one sentence stating which prevails if they differ. That is a drafting choice, not a legal requirement, and it prevents an argument later.

## Keeping it accurate

**Tie policy review to adding a vendor.** A new analytics tool, crash reporter or AI API changes what the policy should say. Put "does this change the privacy policy?" on the checklist for adding a dependency. Apple lets you update your privacy details at any time without a new app build.

**Version and date it.** Saudi Article 11(1)(d) of the Implementing Regulations asks for consent documented in a way you can verify later, which in practice means knowing which policy version a user saw. Give each version an identifier and a date; see the [Saudi PDPL developer guide](/en/blog/pdpl-compliance-guide) on consent records.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a Saudi privacy policy have to be in Arabic?

SDAIA's guideline does not name a language. It requires clear, easy, non-misleading wording suited to the people whose data you collect, and recommends the language suited to that audience. For an app aimed at Saudi users, that means Arabic in practice; publishing English alongside it is a business choice.

### What must a privacy policy contain under the Saudi PDPL?

Article 12 lists the purpose of collection, the data collected, how it is collected and stored, how it is processed and destroyed, and the data subject's rights and how to exercise them. SDAIA's guideline adds your official name and activities, contact details, DPO details where required, legal basis, recipients, storage locations, retention, security measures and how to complain to the competent authority.

### Does an app with no accounts and no personal data need a privacy policy?

Yes for Google Play, which requires a policy even for apps that access no personal or sensitive data. Apple lists the privacy policy URL as a required field in App Store Connect. And if you use analytics or crash reporting, you are probably collecting device identifiers anyway.

### Can the privacy policy be a PDF or sit only on the store listing?

Not on Google Play: the policy must be at a public, active, non-geofenced URL and cannot be a PDF, and there must be a link or text inside the app. Apple also requires an easily accessible link inside the app, not only in App Store Connect.

### Do I have to name each third-party vendor?

Neither SDAIA's guideline nor the store rules require vendor names; they require you to describe who receives data. Naming vendors is clearer for users and enterprise buyers, but the list then has to change whenever your vendors do. A data map makes that a quick edit.

### How fast must I answer a user's request made under the policy?

Under Article 3 of the Saudi Implementing Regulations, within 30 days, extendable by up to 30 more if the request needs unusual effort and you tell the user in advance with reasons. Build the request flow so you can meet that window.
