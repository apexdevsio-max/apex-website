# Integrating Gulf Digital Identity: Nafath, UAE Pass, and QDI

**Integrating Nafath (Saudi Arabia) or UAE PASS (UAE) gives your product a government-verified identity — a real name and national ID number — rather than just proof that someone controls an account.** On UAE PASS, the Emirates ID number and full Arabic name come back only for verified accounts. The engineering is a few weeks; the onboarding with the operator is the part to start first. For regulated or KYC-heavy products it is often worth it; for an ordinary consumer app it usually is not.

> Written from an engineering perspective, not legal advice. Current as of November 2026.

This guide covers what integration involves and how to decide whether you need it. If you are scoping a [mobile app](/en/services/mobile-apps) that may need verified sign-in, read the "Do you actually need it?" section before anything else.

---

## What these systems are

| | **Saudi Arabia** | **UAE** | **Qatar** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Identity app | Nafath | UAE PASS | QDI (Qatar Digital ID) |
| Role | National single sign-on | National digital identity | Digital identity wallet |
| Public developer documentation | Not publicly available; request from the operator | [docs.uaepass.ae](https://docs.uaepass.ae/resources/attributes-list) | Not publicly available; ask the Ministry of Interior |

**Nafath** is Saudi Arabia's national sign-in app, used for government portals and many private services. We could not find a public developer portal for it, so the integration route, eligibility and fees are questions to put to the operator directly — not to a reseller.

**UAE PASS** is the UAE's national digital identity. Its [documentation](https://docs.uaepass.ae/resources/attributes-list) lists three capabilities: authentication and digital signature/eSeal (open to government entities and private organisations), and data and document sharing (private organisations).

**QDI** is the Qatari Ministry of Interior's digital identity app. Whether a private app can use it for sign-in, and on what terms, is a question for the ministry.

**The pattern is the same everywhere:** the user approves the request in the national app, and your service receives verified identity attributes rather than credentials you have to store and protect.

## Verified identity versus authentication

This distinction decides whether integration is worth it.

**Ordinary authentication** — email and password, or social login — proves someone controls an account. It says nothing about who they are.

**National identity integration** returns **verified identity**. UAE PASS shows how this works in practice: its [account types page](https://docs.uaepass.ae/quick-start-guide-uae-pass-staging-environment/user-account-types) describes SOP1 accounts as **unverified** (email and mobile checked by one-time code, no Emirates ID), while SOP2 and SOP3 are **verified** against the Emirates ID. The [attributes list](https://docs.uaepass.ae/resources/attributes-list) follows that line:

| Attribute | SOP1 (basic) | SOP2 / SOP3 (verified) |
|---|---|---|
| UUID, email, mobile, English first/last name | Yes | Yes |
| Emirates ID number (`idn`) | No | Yes |
| Full name in Arabic and English, nationality, gender | No | Yes |

**The engineering consequence:** if your use case depends on the Emirates ID number, your code must handle a SOP1 user who signs in successfully but returns no `idn`. Decide up front whether you reject them, ask them to upgrade their account, or let them continue with limited access.

**What verified identity unlocks:**
- **Remote KYC** without document upload and manual review
- **Licence conditions** that require identity verification — check what your own regulator or licence says, for example in [fintech](/en/blog/fintech-sama-compliance) or [health](/en/blog/health-apps-uae-compliance)
- **Fraud reduction** — one national ID, one account
- **Guardian verification** for [children's data consent](/en/blog/childrens-data-protection)
- **Document signing**, where the provider supports it (UAE PASS signing requires a verified account)

**What it does not give you:** a lighter compliance burden. You are now storing national ID numbers. Saudi Arabia's [Personal Data Protection Law](https://dgp.sdaia.gov.sa/wps/portal/pdp/knowledgecenter/details/PDPL) names "personal identity number" as an example of personal data (Article 1), but does **not** list it among the categories of sensitive data, which are racial or ethnic origin, belief, security and criminal data, biometric data, genetic data, health data and data indicating unknown parentage. So it is ordinary personal data in law — but a leaked national ID number is a fraud tool, and it deserves the same storage discipline as sensitive data.

## Do you actually need it?

It is worth it when:

- Your **licence or regulator requires** verified identity
- You are building a **government or quasi-government** service
- **Remote KYC** is a core flow, and the alternative is manual document review
- **Duplicate accounts** are a material fraud problem
- You need **legally meaningful signatures** and the provider offers signing

It is usually **not** worth it for ordinary e-commerce, content products, consumer apps with no verification requirement, or MVPs still validating demand. There you add an onboarding dependency and ID-number storage for a benefit users did not ask for.

**A pragmatic middle path:** ship with conventional authentication, design your identity layer so a verified provider can be added later (store an external identity link per user, not just a password hash), and integrate when a real requirement appears.

## Onboarding is the long pole

The technical integration is a standard OAuth 2.0 authorisation-code flow: UAE PASS documents it as [obtaining an authorisation code](https://docs.uaepass.ae/feature-guides/authentication/web-application/1.-obtaining-the-oauth2-access-code), then an access token, then the user's information — and recommends the `state` parameter against CSRF. **Getting onboarded is what takes calendar time.**

UAE PASS describes its [service provider onboarding](https://docs.uaepass.ae/getting-onboarded-with-uae-pass/onboarding-process-for-uae-pass-service-providers) in four phases: **initiation, development, assessment, go-live**. It also provides a staging environment with test accounts, so you can build before production access is granted.

**Questions to put to the operator before you plan dates** (for Nafath and QDI we found no public answer, so ask in writing):

1. Is a locally registered entity required, and is our sector eligible?
2. What documents and security evidence does the application need?
3. How long does each phase usually take, and who signs off the assessment?
4. Are there access fees, per-transaction fees, or neither?
5. Which attributes will we receive, and can we limit the request to only what we need?

**The scheduling consequence:** start the application at the same time as design, and build against staging in parallel. Treating approval as a task that follows development is how launches slip.

## Technical patterns that matter

**Redirect-based flow.** Your app hands off to the identity provider, the user approves in the national app, and control returns with an authorisation code. Design session handling around a round trip out of your app and back.

**Request and store only what you need.** If you only need to know a user is a verified UAE resident, you may not need to persist the Emirates ID number at all — a verified flag plus the provider's UUID can be enough. If you do store the number, encrypt it at field level and restrict who can read it.

**Handle the unverified user.** A SOP1 UAE PASS login succeeds but lacks the ID attributes. Make that a deliberate path, not a null-pointer error.

**Design for failure.** The user abandons the flow, the national app is not installed, the service is briefly unavailable, or the session times out. "The national app is not installed" deserves real handling rather than an error message.

**Do not make it the only route** unless a rule requires it. Visitors, foreign residents without the national app, and users who simply prefer email still need a way in.

**Test the Arabic flow.** The user leaves your app and returns. If your app is Arabic and right-to-left, check the whole round trip in Arabic — see the [Arabic localisation guide](/en/blog/arabic-localization-guide).

## Effort

Once you have staging access, the integration itself is modest:

| Work | Effort |
|---|---|
| Authorisation flow and token handling | 5 – 12 days |
| Unverified-account, failure and edge-case handling | 3 – 5 days |
| Attribute minimisation, encrypted storage and access control | 2 – 4 days |
| Operator assessment and go-live | Set by the operator |

Engineering time is billed at the agency rates in our published rate card: **$50–100 per hour as of November 2026** (about SAR 200–400 or AED 200–400). The dominant cost is usually calendar time in onboarding, not developer days — which is why it belongs at the start of the schedule.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which UAE PASS accounts return the Emirates ID number?

Only verified accounts. The UAE PASS attributes list returns the `idn` (Emirates ID number), full Arabic and English name, nationality and gender for SOP2 and SOP3 accounts. Basic SOP1 accounts, which are unverified, return only a UUID, email, mobile number and English first and last name.

### Can a private company integrate UAE PASS?

Yes for authentication and digital signature: the UAE PASS documentation lists both as available to government entities and private organisations, and data and document sharing as available to private organisations. Access still goes through the operator's onboarding phases — initiation, development, assessment and go-live.

### Is there a public developer portal for Nafath?

We could not find one as of November 2026. Eligibility, the application route, fees and timelines for Nafath should be confirmed directly with the operator, in writing, before you commit to a launch date.

### Is a national ID number "sensitive data" under Saudi PDPL?

Not by the law's definition. Article 1 of the PDPL gives the personal identity number as an example of personal data, and the sensitive-data list does not include it. Treat it with sensitive-grade controls anyway — field-level encryption, restricted access, and a clear purpose — because a leaked ID number enables fraud.

### Should national identity be the only login method?

Only if a rule or your licence requires it. Visitors, residents without the national app, and users with foreign documents still need access. Offer conventional authentication alongside, and design what a user can do with each.

### How much engineering does one integration take?

Roughly 10 to 21 working days for one provider once you have staging access: the authorisation flow, handling of unverified accounts and failures, and minimised encrypted storage. Onboarding and the operator's assessment run on the operator's schedule, on top of that.

## Before you commit

**Integrate for verified identity, not for convenience.** If you only need to know that someone controls an account, conventional authentication is simpler and carries fewer obligations.

**Start the operator conversation before you need it,** and write down the answers to the five questions above.

**And keep a fallback path,** so a product built around one national identity system does not shut out users who have every right to be there.
